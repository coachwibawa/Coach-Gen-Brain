# Coach Gen Writing Rulebook
One rulebook for everything written in your voice: articles, letters, chapters, key moments and case studies. It replaces the Language Rules, the Book Rules and the writing rules spread across skills and memory. Your quotes and the evidence behind each rule stay with the rule in the rules table, not here.
Tier 0 and Tier 1 fail a draft. Tier 2 is polish and never fails one. The writer loads Tier 0, Tier 1, its output's card and the Tier 2 rules that card names. A script checks the marks, and one checker per Tier 1 rule reads the whole draft.
Lines are numbered straight through, so any line can be marked by its number. Nothing goes live until you say go.
## Tier 0: never in a delivered draft
### Banned marks and words
- No em dashes. Use a full stop, comma, colon or brackets.
- No semicolons.
- No emojis.
- Never "my clients". Say "someone I coach" or "the people I coach", or the person's name.
- Never the business "we" or "our team" (the company speaking). The shared human "we" is fine.
### His words stay his
- What he wrote by hand keeps his voice. Text he typed, and lines he marked as keepers, keep his wording and tense.
- A fix to his text (tense, grammar, a clearer word) is a proposal he accepts or rejects, never applied on its own.
- Before proposing a change to the punctuation around his words, work out what each phrase is doing there (an item in a list, a lead-in, a quote), so the fix never changes his meaning.
- The brain holds the truth of what happened and what was said. How a recorded line is quoted on the page follows "A quote earns its marks" (Tier 1).
- Locked names and lines are spelled exactly: Lasting Change, the 5 Laws of Lasting Change, Reset My Course, Reframe Past Regret, Resolve Present Friction, Reap Future Habits, Relax To Results, the 3 Stages of Consistency (Consistent Effort, Momentum Engine, Effortless Result), the Energy Audit, "If you keep trying, but never finish, this is why.", and "Every time you fail to make change, you succeed at staying who you are."
### True material only
- Every fact comes from the brain or his published pieces. A transcript only checks a fact, never adds one.
- Everything in the brain is real unless its certainty says built. Never ask him to verify it.
- Never invent his biography, a title, a subtitle, a citation, a claim about a book, or any fact under his name. When a piece needs a detail from his life that the brain lacks, ask him.
- Missing material is marked [MATERIAL NEEDED: what, and where it would go], never filled.
- His words and Claude's wording stay apart. Never present Claude's idea as his philosophy.
- A book is checked before it is quoted: names, terms, years and claims are verified, never recalled.
### His method and his thesis
- The method, and an article's closing third, come from the Law's own material in the brain: its Core Exercise, Hidden Reward, self-directed questions and DoD lines.
- Argue his thesis, never the genre's and never a book author's. An invention that fits well is still a failure.
### Built details: the hard lines
- In a story about someone he coaches, the core stays true: what they were stuck on, what they did, what changed, and which Law it shows.
- Never a better or invented result, or raised stakes: a setback, loss or threat the record does not show. A persona invented only to show a point may get a result.
- Never the person's past (childhood, family history, why they became who they are) unless they told him.
- Never move an insight or a line from the person to someone else.
- Never family members acting or speaking. A family event offstage, used as a cause, can pass.
- Never sensory detail from a scene he was not in.
- Never what no real person would do.
- In research, never a made-up participant, an unchecked number, or a feeling given to the researchers.
- Health is fine when the record holds it. A built health detail (a condition, diagnosis, medicine, reading or weight the record does not hold) is never added.
- Money only when it shows a struggle or a human side, never as a flex or a promise of wealth.
- His own stories follow the same lines. A true event may move to another day. Never a built action of his own, never his family.
### Quotes: the hard lines
- Never put a claim, motive or feeling in a person's mouth that none of their records supports.
- Never make a person sharper, wittier or wiser than their records show: no wording they would never use, no new beats, no author-grade images.
- Never drop a word the person owns: their image, their signature phrase.
### Print names and hidden people
- Print names come only from brain.personas. Never a real client's name.
- Never a detail that identifies the person. Anything marked never use stays out.
### His stance
- Everyone is resourceful. The reader and the people he coaches are not broken: their power is pointed the wrong way or doubted, not missing.
- He is the editor who helps someone rewrite their story, never the hero who saves them.
- Never pitying, diagnosing or know-it-all. No labels for the reader ("lazy", "broken", "your anxiety").
## Tier 1: core craft (a break fails the draft)
### One thread
- Before drafting, fill in "this piece is about ____" and the one thought the reader leaves with.
- Both go at the top of the draft as one grey line, so he reads the draft against what it aimed for.
- Every paragraph is a step on that thread and helps the reader relate to their own ____. The second half is tested too: a supporting idea never takes it over.
- An idea, story or quote another chapter owns (Book_Status.md) gets one callback line at most.
- Anything off the thread is cut, not parked, however true or interesting.
### Joins answer the reader's question
- Between every two paragraphs the reader has a one-line question. The next paragraph's first sentence answers it.
- Every beat is joined to the one before. The reader never has to guess the link.
- A switch of person or mode (story to "you", third person to first) gets a bridge sentence that names the turn and its reason.
- The join from a story to its takeaway finishes the story first, then turns.
### Links are real
- Each sentence takes something from the one before: a repeated key word, a pronoun with one clear referent, a question and its answer, a time word, a "but" at a real turn.
- "So", "then", "because" and "but" claim true relations. A false "so" fails.
- Repeat the words, never a far "it" or "this". When the reader must recall a sentence, give its words again. You hold context far longer than he or a reader does, so an "it" that is clear to you is often lost to them.
- Every sentence has an owner. No passive that hides who did it.
- A sentence that only says the last one again, louder, is cut. Test: delete it, and if only volume is lost, it was a repeat.
### Few names, one each
- List the terms and names the reader must carry out of the piece. Keep 2 or 3.
- Each keeps the same words every time. No second name for a named thing.
- Labels from the source (Act 1, Insight 2, "the pattern") are not the reader's words. Say the thing plainly.
- A name the reader need not keep (a researcher, a YouTuber) appears once with a role, or not at all.
### Nothing arrives bare
- Show it, then name it, then say it plainly. Nothing is named before the reader has seen it.
- A surprising idea comes with one of these beside it: an example the reader has lived, its reason in the next sentence, premises the reader already accepts, or the reader getting there first.
- A new idea is explained in plain words. Imagery is never spent on it.
- One leap of logic per step. List the steps before drafting, give each its own paragraph, and write every step the argument needs. Never merge steps to stay short.
- Each paragraph leans only on ideas the reader brought or met earlier in the piece.
### Images from the reader's life
- An image comes from the reader's own life, his life, the person's own words, or a story everyone knows. Never a made-up metaphor.
- One image at a time. Never two image systems in one passage. Pay it off in one plain line.
### One story, told once
- One arc, told forward. You can say in one sentence which story you are telling.
- A person's story is told whole in one place, including the part that pays off later. After that they return only as a callback: the same words, a new layer, no new scene.
- No return trips to a story already told, and no new pieces of it later.
- A cut arc is never replaced by another arc. The hole usually wants stakes.
- Close every loop you open by carrying the timeline on, never by announcing the ending early.
### Stakes, not more story
- When a beat is weak, raise what is at risk. Never add narrative.
- Raise stakes to what they really are, concrete and plural. A claimed danger gets a clause that shows the danger.
### Warm the water
- Before a client's line, give the facts the reader lacks, never the meaning. Background is short and comes first.
- A one-sentence quote gets a longer setup, or runs a little longer itself, so it carries its own relatability.
- His records were said inside hours of shared context. Every leap that context took for granted is served on the page, over paragraphs if needed.
- A gap between two recorded moments gets one or two reasonable bridges from the allowed list.
### A quote earns its marks
- The goal: a quoted person sounds like the capable person they are, to a reader who was not in the room.
- Why it matters: in a session, English may not be the person's first language, they may be overwhelmed, and they are often putting into words something they never thought about before. Several of these can be true at once. Said aloud, with the session around it, that is fine. In print, with no context, it reads as choppy fragments.
- First ask whether the line should be a quote at all. Quote a feeling, the person's view, an image, a phrase they own, or the turn or payoff. Report facts and logistics in prose.
- Then read it cold, after its setup only. If a capable person would say it and it makes sense without the session, keep it as said, cleaned of filler and grammar slips.
- If not, rebuild it only as far as it needs: think each sentence through, then find the words the session held. Use the person's own words first, from any of their records. Two lines from one session may join into one quote. Stance words they never said ("In my head") only where a record supports that stance.
- Every quote does a job: a contrast, a turn, or a payoff.
- Thinking out loud is never quoted as said.
- A verbatim quote that fails the cold read fails the draft, the same as a bad rebuild. Keeping a line as said is never the safe choice by default.
- His own lines follow the same rule.
- Every rebuilt quote is listed in the end note: the record's words, what changed, and the record each borrowed word came from.
### Bring it to the reader's life
- After a case or idea from one area of life, show how it reaches the reader's other areas, often by shotgunning several quick examples.
### Research backs, never interrupts
- The idea comes first, in his words. Then the study in 2 to 5 sentences: who was studied, what was done, what happened. One finding. Then its meaning in one plain line.
- In the prose, no year and no university, and at most one name with a role the reader can use ("the psychologist Carol Dweck"). Otherwise "researchers" or "in one study". Full source details go in the end note, and in an article's Insight 2.
- Never inside his story's timeline. A study waits until the story ends or the piece turns to the reader.
- The reader's words, not the paper's. The facts stay true, the presentation is free.
- A study gets only the sentences its step needs. Quote the original work a book cites, not the book, and never over-quote the reference books.
- Nothing is added for protection. A caveat passes the same reader test as any other line, or it goes. Where the frame is his, it is marked by what it did to him, never by what the study does not prove.
- Outside voices: name and role in the prose, page and full source in the end note. Always their idea, never his.
### Built details serve the reader
- The record is the default for what happened. Build only to answer a question the reader will ask, or to hide the person.
- Each built detail does work for the reader. One that only decorates is cut, even when allowed.
- Where a record allows several builds, choose the one or two that do the most, and ask whether you can picture this person doing it.
- Allowed only when the condition in brackets holds, otherwise left out: an action built from something the person only said (only a small, ordinary action that carries the talk, never a staged action that proves a point), a feeling they did not name (only as his hunch, earned by their own words), and another person's act (only when it does not change the tone of the story). A session number or an exact time the record does not hold is never added.
- Use very carefully: a line built for him (it must fit him and be the best way to make the point), his built first move (only a question, or what a coach would do), what the person did after the session, "almost everyone I coach" with three quick examples, his observations (one thing noticed, then a question, never three stacked into a judgement), and Singlish or other local words.
- A detail not on the allowed list is left out and asked about in the end note. Every built detail is listed in the end note next to what the record says.
- After building, read the whole passage again as the reader would.
### No meta-narration
- Never explain the piece's shape ("two lessons, 10 years apart"). In the book, a signpost the reader needs is fine ("But first, some background").
### Rhythm without choppiness
- A short sentence has a job: to compress, turn, reveal, answer, or hold a beat. Never to bring a new fact.
- One event, one sentence. A long sentence may hold a sequence in time order.
- After 3 or 4 sentences of the same shape, write one that moves the idea on in a new shape. No runs of clipped fragments.
- Fragments: none by default. A deliberate one is flagged in the end note for his call.
### Parallels that truly match
- Parallel only for items that truly match. Keep the subject parallel: both "the", or both "you".
- A mirror (a case, then his own story) needs matching beats and enough material on both sides. Otherwise, no mirror.
### Plain words he would say
- Prefer concrete nouns. Explain ideas plainly.
- Use words he would fully understand on first read: his comprehension is the bar for his readers. A few strong, concrete words are welcome, never a general lift in register.
- Plain, not crafted, where the piece explains how something works.
- A call, session or talk is rewritten as prose in his voice, never copied in the order it was said. Each quoted line is as well written as the prose around it.
### Protect the author
- Being open and vulnerable is welcome, often good.
- Out: a line that reads against him out of context, such as a bad practice or a wealth flex.
- A moment that shows him unfinished sits clearly in the past, unless it pays off a vulnerable build.
- Recognition, never credit: when his practice shows up in someone else's work, it is a surprise, never "I did it first".
- A line marked /privileged keeps its fact, is made acceptable, and says plainly where it was a privilege.
### Turns and endings
- A section rises to one turn, then hands off: hook, build, turn, one hand-off line. Examples and proof come before the turn.
- At the turn, prescriptive, not philosophical.
- An article or letter never ends on the hard question alone: a concrete possibility follows, and it promises liberation, not survival.
- No teacher's summary in the prose. A chapter ends on the relationship, an echo, or one sayable line.
### AI tells
- No "Stop X. Start Y." pairs.
- No "not just X. Y." where the second half only repeats the first, louder.
- No templated headlines ("The [Noun] Nobody [Verb]", "The Truth About X"), clichés, corporate jargon or hype (transform your life, unlock your potential, level up, game-changer, journey, hack, guru, secret).
- No clipped, over-simple prose stripped of names and numbers.
- Only bust a myth almost every reader already holds. No cleverness the audience will not recognise.
## Tier 2: polish (never fails a draft)
### Mechanics, fixed silently
- British spelling. "Everyday" is one word only as an adjective ("an everyday habit"). As an adverb it is "every day".
- Oxford comma.
- Numbers the books' way: small counts in words (three times, two months), digits for measures, percentages, years and labels (a 30-minute walk, Chapter 3, Law 1).
- In Claude's own text, grammar and spelling are fixed silently, in his voice: tense (has and had, was and were), agreement and fragments. In his text, they are proposals (Tier 0).
- Contractions where the line is spoken or warm.
### Length and numbers are guardrails
- Length follows the material. Never pad, never compress steps.
- The script reports the word count and reading grade. They catch gross failure only and never prove a draft good.
### Specific detail
- Name the books, places, brands, people and exact numbers.
- A place name gets its category ("Kibale National Park, Uganda").
### Quoted self-talk
- The reader's inner voice goes in quotes, in first person ("It won't feel like me").
- Quotes sit inside the sentence by default. A quoted line may stand on its own for effect.
- When a quoted line moves into a first-person passage, its "you" may become "I" ("You should never give up" becomes "I will never give up"). A slogan whose punch is in its exact words keeps them.
### Lists of three
- A list of three spans different areas of life, never three takes on one.
- The first two items are in the reader's words. The third calls back to the story, so the aha lands there.
- Vary the counts. Twos and fours work too. Sometimes even fives when we’re really talking about a broad term.
### Author presence
- Warmth comes from his own short story, admitting something, mirrored on the case it follows. Only from the brain, where one fits, never to fill a slot.
- Flat claims where the evidence is solid. A hedge only at a real limit. Verdicts in the first person.
- Check in with the reader: answer the question they are already asking, and say their doubt before they raise it.
### Callbacks and staying close
- Once the piece turns to the reader, the next paragraphs open on the reader's life, not the far-off frame.
- After a story ends, the second half calls back to it at least once.
- A thing comes back in the exact words the reader first met, with a short tag. Each callback adds a layer.
### People on the page
- A case: one sentence gives the person and what is at stake. The situation comes before they speak. An action or a feeling comes right before a short quote. Then one sentence of meaning, using a word planted earlier.
- Two or three exchanges are told in prose. Quote the turns that carry feeling, report the turns that carry facts, use plain verbs (asked, went on, pressed), and put something human between the lines.
- A coaching scene is warm and shows him as a good coach. "In a session" alone reads as a transaction.
- Set up a scene with the body, setting only where it carries feeling, the inner voice, and one confident chain of cause and effect.
### Built details you may use
- A cause between two recorded events, if real life works that way, even one that makes the person look weak.
- What an unnamed thing was, and what usually happens next.
- Small facts the person could have told him: when, what, how long.
- Small things he saw in the session: a pause, a silence, where they looked, how loud they said it.
- A plain scene of the problem that serves the reader.
- The person's own odd fixes, if plausible. People solve their problems in their own odd ways: raising chickens, wearing a tie to work every day, or David's daily score of 0, half or 1 point, with money owed to a friend when it ran too low.
- A result that matches the record, told as a light scene.
- His narration taking the person's image a step further, or explaining their move.
- Swaps that hide the person: the object, the job, the meeting. Every other detail stays consistent with the swap. The role changes rarely.
- In research: a scene the reader is asked to picture ("Picture yourself in that room").
## Cards
### Chapter (5 Laws of Lasting Change)
- Point of view: he is the author "I", telling other people's stories and his own, speaking to the reader as "you".
- The one thought carries the whole chapter. A pattern to reach for: sections plant arguments that a later section joins into one picture.
- Many people, not one. He has coached tens of people, but the records go deep for only a few, so no one persona carries the book. Material is spread across print names where a hiding swap allows it, and quick examples from other people show the range.
- Parents appear among the examples (worry about their children, parenting), for credibility and reach.
- A recurring feature of the book: each person beats the same pattern with their own odd fix. When the record holds one, show it.
- The opening story has no heading. A heading marks a turn to a new idea, never a mid-thread break and never an act of the story. It names the idea by default, asks a question only where the reader is truly asking it, and is fresh and human, sometimes a pun.
- Each chapter opens where the last left the reader. A chapter is named only when the reader must go back and use a tool.
- Default shape, a menu and not a sequence: the opening story, the stuck point in the reader's life, myth and reality, the idea shown then named, the method (his Core Exercise, never invented), what changes, why this Law comes here and what it opens, the From Within You box, the Chapter Summary box.
- An article's Insight 2 and 3 are optional in a chapter. Kept questions go in From Within You.
- The Chapter Summary box is never in "I". It holds the chapter's key phrases, and every line comes from the chapter.
- Only the line the chapter is built to deliver is set apart (the pull line). Most chapters have one, some none.
- A figure is a tool or model the reader will use: numbered by chapter (Figure 3.1), with a one-sentence caption, pointed to once, never in place of the prose.
- The flagship exercises (the thought map, the page and its marks) are alluded to and built up before they arrive.
- The Introduction is written last and promises only what the chapters deliver. It opens like Dweck's: a short origin scene with a person in it, his standing, the promise to "you", then what is inside, with the anonymity line. The subtitle is never repeated in the opening pages.
- No conclusion unless he asks.
- The end note (grey, after a page break, starting "From Claude,") holds the [MATERIAL NEEDED] list, every built detail beside its record, any deliberate fragment, at most 3 questions about facts or missing material, and the exact phrase and full source of every study or book cited.
- Paragraphs are indented with no gap, as in the printed book.
- Tier 2 rules loaded: Mechanics, Length, Specific detail, Quoted self-talk, Lists of three, Author presence, Callbacks and staying close, People on the page, Built details you may use.
### Key moments
- The 5 or 6 places that carry a chapter: its opening, its turns, its payoff, its hand-off.
- Each moment is written three ways, built differently and not reworded (time order, his line first, the reader first, the payoff first, a callback, a mirror), and each version is labelled with how it is built.
- Every fact comes from the chapter's fact sheet. Every version follows the chapter card.
- Delivered in a Google Doc, "5 Laws Manuscript, <Chapter> Key Moments", with one grey line at the top asking him to pick or mix one version per moment, and a visible gap after every paragraph.
- Tier 2 rules loaded: the chapter card's.
