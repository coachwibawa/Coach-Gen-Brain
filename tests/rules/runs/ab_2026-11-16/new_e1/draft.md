## Cutting What Still Matters

At one point, all of these were in my life at the same time: three side hustles with three different relatives, walking my dog every morning, free coaching for friends who needed it but couldn't pay, maintaining my business website, an entrepreneur community, my BNI membership, tending my rose garden, going to a gym that was a 45-minute drive away, keeping up my Japanese by talking to native speakers online, being a great evaluator at Toastmasters, the hangout communities I was part of, my friend group chats, and the Inside the Introvert Community. Every one of them was important to me. Each was a project I had nurtured from zero to get it to where it was.

But all of it added up, 5 minutes here and 45 minutes there, and there were 20 of these in my life at any given time. My time tracker showed the total: every day, 2 to 3 hours went to the mundane side of these things, and to just keeping them all on top of my working memory.

The hours were only part of the cost. Little by little, my list had crept to the point that every month was filled with the same things, with no room for new opportunities. The question that moved me was a simple one: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" The honest answer was no.

So at the start of one year, I began to cut, because to take control of my life, I need to take control of my day. My BNI membership was one of the cuts, and so was the entrepreneur community. I call this pruning, and it is different from weeding. When you weed, you pull out what is useless. When you prune, you cut back something that is still alive.

That is why killing hurts. Of all these things, I could never say that any one of them was of no use to my life and well-being. I didn't start my Japanese fluent: I had nurtured it from zero, and now that it had finally borne fruit, neglecting it felt really bad. Nor could I say for sure that any of them was a fruitless endeavour, or that one of the side hustles would not turn into a million-dollar business. I pruned anyway.

Only after I killed them could I go back to them with a clear mind, one year later. Some of what I had pruned, I realised I actually needed in my life, and those I restarted from a fresh seed. Being a great evaluator at Toastmasters had been on my list. When I went back to Toastmasters, I gave an evaluation I was proud of. This time, I was not squeezing it in between 20 other things.

I have room for more commitments now that I can 100% commit.

I am telling you about my list because I would bet you have one too. Yours might hold a weekly class you joined two years ago, a group chat you read every night, a side project you keep alive with one hour on Sundays, and the school committee you said yes to because your child is at that school. None of them is useless. That is exactly why they stay. A list like that is present friction at its quietest: nothing stands in your way, and still nothing new gets in.

So look at your own month and ask yourself the question that moved me: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" If the answer is no, prune. Pick something that still matters to you and cut it. Don't wait for it to become useless first, because you may never be able to call any of them useless.

Killing hurts, and it will hurt you too. But what you truly need, you can go back to with a clear mind and restart from a fresh seed, the way I did. And this time, there will be room in your month to give it 100%.
