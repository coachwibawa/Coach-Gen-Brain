Draft X

Pruning to Make Room

By the start of the year, my days were full, and almost everything in them mattered to me. I had a side business with a relative, a second one with a different relative, and a third with yet another. I walked my dog every morning, tended my rose garden, and went to a gym that was a 45-minute drive away. I coached, for free, friends who needed it but couldn't pay. I kept my business website up to date, and I kept my Japanese alive by talking to native speakers online. At Toastmasters, the public speaking club, I worked at being a great evaluator, the member who gives feedback on other people's speeches. I was a member of BNI (a business networking group) and of an entrepreneur community. On top of that came a few communities where I simply hung out, my friends' group chats, and the Inside the Introvert Community.

Not one of these came to me ready-made. I had grown each of them from zero to where it was. I did not start my Japanese fluent, and now that it had finally borne fruit, it felt really bad to neglect it.

But all of it added up. It was 5 minutes here and 45 minutes there, and at any given time there were about twenty of these in my life. My time tracker showed the same thing every day: 2 to 3 hours went to these small, everyday things, and to simply keeping all of them in my head.

None of them stood in my way on its own. Together, they had crept up until every month was filled with the same things, and there was no room left for new opportunities. The question that finally moved me was a plain one: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" The honest answer was no.

So at the start of the year, I pruned. My BNI membership went, and so did the entrepreneur community, my place as an evaluator at Toastmasters, and others on the list. To take control of my life, I needed to take control of my day.

Pruning sounds gentle. It did not feel gentle. It felt like killing, and killing hurts. I could not say any of these things was of no use to my life and well-being. I could not even say for sure that one of the side businesses would never turn into a million-dollar business. Every one of them had a real reason to stay.

Only after the pruning did I find a clear mind. A year later, with that clear mind, I went back to the things I had pruned. The ones I realised I actually needed in my life, I started again from a fresh seed.

Toastmasters was one of them, and before the pruning it had been just one more thing to keep in my head. When I went back, I gave an evaluation I was proud of, and this is how I put it: "I have room for more commitments now that I can 100% commit." Pruning had not left me doing less. It had given me room to take on more, and to mean it.

You may not have a rose garden or a dog to walk, but you probably have your own list. It might be a weekly class you still go to because you always have, a group chat you read every night so nobody feels ignored, or a thing you grew from zero and cannot say for sure will never turn into something big.

A list like that never arrives at once. We say yes to one more thing, and then one more, until every month looks the same. I call it overcommitting to new seeds before the existing ones bear fruit. Each thing is small, and each thing is good. If any one of them were useless, pruning would be easy. It hurts because you are pruning good things, and the good things are what fill the month.

So ask yourself the question that moved me: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" If the honest answer is no, something good has to go. And whatever you find you truly need, you can plant again later, from a fresh seed and with a clear mind.

Draft Y

Making Room

At one point, all of these were in my life at the same time: three side hustles with three different relatives, walking my dog every morning, free coaching for friends who needed it but couldn't pay, maintaining my business website, an entrepreneur community, my BNI membership (a business networking group), tending my rose garden, going to a gym that was a 45-minute drive away, keeping up my Japanese by talking to native speakers online, being a great evaluator at Toastmasters (a public speaking club), the hangout communities I was part of, my friend group chats, and the Inside the Introvert Community. Every one of them was important to me. Each was a project I had nurtured from zero to get it to where it was.

But all of it added up, 5 minutes here and 45 minutes there, and there were 20 of these in my life at any given time. My time tracker showed the total: every day, 2 to 3 hours went to running these things, and to just keeping them all on top of my working memory.

The hours were only part of the cost. Little by little, my list had crept to the point that every month was filled with the same things, with no room for new opportunities. The question that moved me was a simple one: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" The honest answer was no.

So at the start of one year, I ended my BNI membership and left the entrepreneur community, because to take control of my life, I need to take control of my day. I call this pruning: ending things that still matter to me, so that my month has room again and whatever stays gets all of me. Pruning means ending them for good, not pausing them for later.

Pruning hurts. Of all these things, I could never say that any one of them was of no use to my life and well-being. I didn't start my Japanese fluent: I had nurtured it from zero, and now that it had finally borne fruit, neglecting it felt really bad. Nor could I say for sure that any of them would never bear fruit, or that one of the side hustles would not turn into a million-dollar business. I pruned anyway.

Only after pruning them could I go back to them with a clear mind, one year later. Some of them, I realised, I actually needed in my life, and those I restarted from a fresh seed. After the pruning, I went back to Toastmasters and gave an evaluation I was proud of, my feedback on another member's speech. This time, I was not squeezing it in between 20 other things.

Pruning had left me with fewer things, and yet with room to take on more. Today I put it this way: "I have room for more commitments now that I can 100% commit."

I am telling you about my list because I would bet you have one too. Yours might hold a weekly class you joined two years ago, a group chat you read every night, a side project you keep alive with one hour on Sundays, and the school committee you said yes to because your child is at that school. None of them is useless. That is exactly why they stay.

So look at your own month and ask yourself the question that moved me: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" If the answer is no, prune. Pick something that still matters to you and prune it. Don't wait for it to become useless first, because you may never be able to call any of them useless.

Pruning hurts, and it will hurt you too. But what you truly need, you can go back to with a clear mind and restart from a fresh seed, the way I did. And this time, there will be room in your month to give it 100%.
