## Pruning to Make Room

By the start of the year, my days were full, and almost everything in them mattered to me. I had a side business with a relative, a second one with a different relative, and a third with yet another. I walked my dog every morning, tended my rose garden, and went to a gym that was a 45-minute drive away. I coached friends who needed it but couldn't pay, for free, and I kept my business website up to date. I kept my Japanese alive by talking to native speakers online. At Toastmasters, the public speaking club, I worked at being a great evaluator, the member who gives feedback on other people's speeches. I was also a member of BNI, a business networking group, and of an entrepreneur community, a few communities I hung out in, my friends' group chats, and the Inside the Introvert Community.

Not one of these came to me ready-made. I had grown each of them from zero to where it was. I did not start my Japanese fluent, and now that it had finally borne fruit, it felt really bad to neglect it.

But all of it added up. It was 5 minutes here and 45 minutes there, and at any given time there were about 20 of these in my life. My time tracker showed the same thing every day: 2 to 3 hours went to these small, everyday things, and to simply keeping all of them in my head.

None of them stood in my way on its own. Together, they had crept up until every month was filled with the same things, and there was no room left for new opportunities. The question that finally moved me was a plain one: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" The honest answer was no.

So at the start of the year, I pruned. My BNI membership went. So did the entrepreneur community, my place as an evaluator at Toastmasters, and others on the list. To take control of my life, I need to take control of my day, and that was where all of them lived.

Pruning sounds gentle. It did not feel gentle. It felt like killing, and killing hurts. I could not say any of these things was of no use to my life and well-being. I could not even say for sure that one of the side businesses would never turn into a million-dollar business. Every one of them had a real reason to stay.

Only after the pruning did I find a clear mind. A year later, I went back with that clear mind and started again, from a fresh seed, the ones I realised I actually needed in my life.

Toastmasters was one of them. Before the pruning, it had been one more thing to keep in my head. When I went back, I gave an evaluation I was proud of, and I could say it plainly: "I have room for more commitments now that I can 100% commit." Pruning had not left me doing less. It had given me room to take on more, and to mean it.

You may not have a rose garden or a dog to walk, but you probably have your own list. It might be a weekly class you still go to because you always have, a group chat you read every night so nobody feels ignored, or a thing you grew from zero and cannot say for sure will never turn into something big.

None of it arrives at once. We say yes to one more thing, and then one more, overcommitting to new seeds before the existing ones bear fruit, as I call it, until every month looks the same. Each thing is small, and each thing is good. That is what makes it so hard to prune. Letting go of what is useless is easy, and it never gives you much room. The room is held by the good things, and the good things are the ones that hurt.

So before you add anything to next month, ask yourself the question that moved me: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" If the honest answer is no, something good has to go. And whatever you find you truly need, you can plant again later, from a fresh seed and with a clear mind.
