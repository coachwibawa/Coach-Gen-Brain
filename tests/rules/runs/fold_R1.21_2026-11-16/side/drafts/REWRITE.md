## Pruning the Good Things

At the start of one year, I set out to take back control of my day. My reason was simple: to take control of my life, I need to take control of my day. But when I looked at what my day was made of, I could not find one thing I wanted to get rid of.

There were three side hustles, each with a different relative. There was my dog to walk every morning, my rose garden to tend, and a gym that was a 45-minute drive away. I coached friends who needed it but could not pay, kept my business website running, and talked with native speakers online so my Japanese would not slip. I tried to be a great evaluator at Toastmasters. I belonged to BNI, a business networking group, and to an entrepreneur community, a few hangout communities, my friend group chats, and the Inside The Introvert community.

Every one of these was something I had grown from zero. Take my Japanese. I was not fluent when I started learning it, and now that it had finally borne fruit, I felt really bad about neglecting it. Each of the others had a story like that behind it.

None of them was the problem on its own. The problem was what they added up to. It was five minutes here and 45 minutes there, and at any time there were about twenty of these in my life. My time tracker showed me the total: two to three hours, every day, went to these mundane things and to just keeping them all on top of my working memory. Not one of them stood in my way. Together, they were the friction in every day I had.

Worse, they had crept up on me until every month was filled with the same things. One question finally moved me: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" The honest answer was no.

So I pruned. Pruning is a gardener's word. A gardener cuts away branches that are still alive, so the plant has room to grow. My BNI membership went, and so did the entrepreneur community, along with others on that list.

Pruning sounds gentle. It felt like killing, and killing hurts. I could not say that any one of those things was of no use to my life and well-being. I could not even say for sure that it was fruitless, that one of the side hustles would never have grown into a million-dollar business. I cut them anyway, because the other choice was another year of months that all looked the same.

Only after I had pruned them did my mind clear. A year later, I went back with that clear mind, and I restarted, with a fresh seed, the ones I realised I actually needed in my life. One of them was Toastmasters. I went back and gave an evaluation I was proud of, and I knew why it had gone so well: "I have room for more commitments now that I can 100% commit." That is the room pruning gives back.

I tell you my story because your list will hold different things from mine, and I would bet it adds up the same way. It might be the parents' group chat for your child's class, an online course you paid for and still mean to finish, a weekly call you keep because you always have, or a side hustle that might, one day, turn into something big. Each one is good. Each one took real effort to grow from zero.

Here is why that is a trap. A bad habit gives you a reason to drop it. A good thing never does, because it is good. So nothing ever leaves your list on its own, and every new good thing you say yes to is added to what is already there. The list only grows, and the month fills up to match it. You can see it in the new thing you keep saving for "next month", the class you want to take or the idea you want to try. Next month arrives already full of the same things as this one, so the new thing waits again.

The easy fix is to keep everything and do each thing a little less. I understand why it is tempting: nothing has to die. But doing a thing less does not take it off your mind. My tracker did not only count the minutes I spent on those twenty things. It counted the time spent keeping them on top of my working memory, and a thing you still hold on to still has to be held. Think of the course you skipped this week. You did not spend an hour on it, but it still came back to you on Sunday night, along with the guilt. As long as all twenty stay on your list, all twenty stay in your head. Only pruning one takes it out.

That makes pruning the costly choice, and I will not make it sound smaller than it is. You will cut things that are still alive. Some of them you grew from zero. One of them might be the side hustle you secretly hoped would grow. When you prune, you give up the chance to find out this month, and that loss is real.

It is worth paying because of what fewer things do to the ones you keep. When twenty things share your mind, each one gets only a small slice of it, and none of them gets your best. When fewer things share it, each gets all of you. That was the difference in my evaluation at Toastmasters: I could finally commit 100%. Picture the one thing in your life that deserves your full attention, your child, your work, your craft. Ask how much of you it gets on a normal evening, when the rest of your list is still running in the background.

You may still be asking the question I asked: "What if I cut the one that would have grown?" You cannot know that in advance, and the reason is the crowding itself. While twenty things fill your mind, they all feel equally needed, because each one is pulling at you at the same time. Only after the cut, once the noise is gone, does real need show itself. That is how it went for me. With a clear mind, a year later, I could see which ones I actually needed, and a pruned thing can be restarted with a fresh seed. Pruning is not losing something forever. It is taking it out long enough to find out whether you need it.

So here is what I ask of you. Ask yourself the question that moved me: "Is there room in this month for anything I have not done before?" If the honest answer is no, choose the good things you will prune, and stop them this week.

What you prune, you can plant again. What you never prune will fill every month with the same things.
