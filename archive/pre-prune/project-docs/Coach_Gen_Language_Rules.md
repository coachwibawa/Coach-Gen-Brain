# Coach Gen Language Rules

The single guardrail document for translating Coach Gen's raw material (worksheet answers, transcripts, talking-out-loud) into finished writing in his voice. It covers both tracks: short-form content (5x5x5 highlights, quote cards, captions, opening lines) and long-form articles (Inside The Introvert).

**This document contains language and voice rules, and the calibration evidence behind them. Nothing else.** Project state, publishing workflow, research provenance and draft history live in `claude/voice-translation-guide.md`, which is the working log.

**Reading the tags.** A rule marked `[short-form]` or `[article]` applies to that track only. Untagged rules apply to everything.

**Maintaining this document.** Add and annotate. Never condense. When a later finding corrects an earlier rule, keep the old line and mark the correction, so the reasoning survives. A rule you delete is a lesson that has to be learned again from scratch.

## The numbers are guardrails, never proof

A draft measured 473 words, 10.8 words per sentence, FK 2.9, ease 94, named things 5.3. His own rewrite of that same draft measured 446, 11.4, FK 3.1, ease 93, named 5.4. **By every metric the two were the same piece. They were not.** Every change he made was invisible to every number.

Use the numbers to catch gross failure. Never as evidence a draft is right.

## Two failure directions, both real

**Too heavy.** Diagnosed on a 626-word draft that scored FK 4.6, easier than his own average. The formula did not detect it. Heaviness comes from length, from the number of turns in the argument, and from abstract nouns. Not from long words.

**Too light.** Diagnosed on two AI drafts at FK 2.4 and 2.2, well under his real 5.0. Over-simplifying, clipping every sentence short and stripping out proper nouns is its own AI signature. It reads bland, not clear. Do not chase a low grade level for its own sake.

## Budget for glue `[article]`

His line-level notes reliably ask for connective tissue, 10 to 25 words each time. One draft grew from 396 to 474 words purely from requested additions. Draft the beats at around 320 so the finished piece lands in range.

**Retired 15 Oct 2026, at his request: no glue budget.** His words: "I don't think glue budget is necessary." The note above stays as a record. The glue rule itself (section 5, "Glue between beats") still applies.

# 2. THE OVERRIDING TEST

His words (2026-09-02):

> "this is not literature review, this is a book, an article. of a coach. and it aims to connect the reader. the article is about \_\_\_\_ and it should stay about \_\_\_\_. you need to only ask, how to make the audience relate with \_\_\_\_"

**Fill the blank before drafting. Then run every beat through one question: does this make the reader relate to their own ____?**

Three beats failed that test across ten drafts of a single piece, each of them interesting, well-sourced and off-topic. A chimpanzee territorial patrol was about acceptance, not regret. A mother-son finding was about maternal bonds, not regret. A grooming detail was about chimpanzee social behaviour, not regret. All three were cut by him.

He has also flagged two lines that survived into an accepted draft as still worth testing: "Regret is not punishment, it is there to stop you repeating something" (an abstract statement, does it connect?) and the grooming detail. **Do not assume a line passed just because it survived.**

**Re-run the blank on the second half.** Drift happens after the turn. In Lasting Change Letters #1 a supporting idea took over the back half. His words: "the theme (which I say from the first prompt) is about negotiating with ourselves. then that should be the main point. shield should be a supporting idea, not the main point of the whole second half."

## The goal is emotional relation

**"the goal is to amplify relate to the audience's emotion, so it's good to show raw emotions and downs."**

The lines he marked "amazing" are small, specific humiliations:

> The crush who laughed. The interview that said they would call back. The thing you built and carried home, and nobody at the table looked up.

**Raw beats tasteful.** He added "Know your place" to a rule that already had two clauses. The two existing clauses described a consequence. His third clause is the thing talking to you, and it is the one that hurts.

# 3. VOICE AND DICTION

**Specificity beats abstraction, always.** "Courage" alone is too vague. **"Courage to do the hard things"** is the actual point.

**Every noun should be something the reader can see, touch, or count.** Abstract nouns are the enemy, not long sentences. This costs zero extra words, which is exactly the ask. The swap is one for one:

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| the introduction we do not make | the hand I do not shake |
| territorial patrols | the most dangerous walk the group takes |
| the affection goes first, the aggression comes after | first they stop grooming him, then they hit him |
| that was the only variable he could see | it was the only thing he could see |
| telling myself I was being reasonable | telling myself I am just being polite |

Words that flagged as too sophisticated and are always replaceable: circuit, variable, consequences, competition, punishment, reputation, acknowledged, concluded, measuring, underneath, particular, reasonable, eventually, alongside, territorial, aggression, affection, introduction, promotion.

**There is no vivid-without-specifics exception.** An earlier rule claimed one, derived from "A Garden Full of Nothing." That piece is AI-written. The exception was deleted.

**What he actually names.** Carol Dweck's Mindset, Daniel Kahneman's Thinking Fast and Slow, Ichiro Kishimi's The Courage to be Disliked, 95.9 FM SmartFM, Moscone West Convention Center, Ray Kroc, McDonalds, a pirated version of Netflix, Starbucks, Angry Birds, Rich Dad Poor Dad, Robert Kiyosaki, James Clear's Atomic Habits, Mbappé, Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham, 3am, 45 minutes, 15kgs, 8 times, 5000 people, 50%, 4th zoom call, chapter 1.

**He writes numbers as digits, not words.** 45 minutes, 3am, 2 weeks, 15 years, 8 times, 10-year-old, 1%. Almost never "forty five." Spelled-out numbers are an instant tell and they cost the specificity the digits carry.

**He names his experts inside the narrative**, not only in a citations section. On Blind Spots: "In 1955, two psychologists named Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham created a model."

**He names the characters.** Use the source's real name where one exists. Where none exists, give one openly ("Call him X") and disclose it in the citation layer rather than passing it off as a field note. An unnamed subject stays a specimen. A named one becomes someone the reader follows.

**Vocabulary check before delivering any draft.** His six true pieces use 769 unique words in 2140 words of text. Diff a draft's unique words against that corpus. One rejected draft used 279 unique words of which 132 appeared nowhere in his published work. Not every unseen word is wrong, and many are innocuous (door, chair, alone). **The signal is unseen abstract nouns of 7 or more letters.** Keep that count low.

**He writes British spelling.** "an empty lecture theatre" in My Dragon. Neighbour, colour, realise. But **"everyday" as one word.**

**Plain over crafted at the mechanism.** "the sentence we read before we speak" became "the sentence we read subconsciously everyday." The first is a made image. The second is how it actually works. When a sentence explains how the thing works, do not decorate it.

Related: "that mistake" became "a similar blunder." The first claimed the human error was literally the animal's. The second is honest, looser, and "blunder" is how a person talks about their own past.

**Name the subject early.** He fused the two words inside the story: "what Melvin has left, is a rule, a regret." Holding the topic word back until the second half makes the piece introduce itself late.

**Corrected (2026-09-25):** name the topic early, not the realization. Stating the realization in paragraph 2 of Lasting Change Letters #1 killed the reveal. See section 5, "When his material is a realization."

**Filler is not one category.** "I guess," "definitely, definitely," "along the lines of" are uncertainty hedges: cut them. "Honestly," "genuinely" are vulnerability and authenticity markers: they can be added or kept.

**Distinguish talking-out-loud from crafted content.** A draft once treated "that's always needed" as a meaningful keyword. It was verbal punctuation, him nodding at his own point, not adding meaning. Confirmed directly: "that's correct." Filter these before building variants around them.

**"Everyday" can be evidence, not just frequency.** Raw line: "explicitly lock away their dream in a drawer, but still think about it everyday." The keyword is not "drawer" (image) or "replay" (mechanism), it is "everyday" as proof the suppression failed. Read for what a recurring detail is evidence of, not just its surface content.

**Powerful words he is open to.** He is not closed to unfamiliar vocabulary: "I am open to learn and have these powerful words, like hazing. and maybe eventually claim them as my own." A small number of strong, concrete, deliberately chosen unfamiliar words are welcome. A general lift in register is not.

## Mechanical, locked

- No em dashes. Use a period, comma, colon or parentheses.
- No semicolons.
- No clichés, no corporate jargon, no emojis.
- Active voice. Address the reader as "you," except in reader self-talk.
- Omit needless words. Concrete over abstract. Mix sentence lengths.
- Sign-off: "Coach Gen."
- **Audience note:** his readers, and his clients, read English as a second language. He is Indonesian, fluent in English shaped by working in Singapore, and many sophisticated English words are ones he would not use in his own writing or daily life.

# 4. PERSON, POV AND SELF-TALK

**First person for the reader's own self-talk, second person for coach address.** "It won't feel like you" is the coach talking about the reader. "It won't feel like me" is the reader's own internal voice. Already the worksheet's own convention, where DoD lines read "I am now..." and "Client can say to themselves."

**Quoted self-talk is a core device.** His note: "I like the 'quotes' to denote self talk, usually a self talk that is relatable to the audience."

It is everywhere in the true corpus:

> "Is this all?" and "This is how I'll find myself" (My Dragon)
> "Do not. Click. That. Button." (My Dragon Finale)
> "I want to be sad" and "You're depressed" (How Do You Reset?)
> six quoted lines in Act 2, including the radio-guru slogans and "What the heck, this is just the start, huh."

**Repeat a quoted line verbatim to bridge two registers.** In his own rewrite the rule appears as a quoted line on its own line, printed twice identically, once for the animal and once for the reader:

> "Do not reach above your standing. You will get hurt. Know your place"

That does the entire bridge with no explanatory sentence. The rejected version said "we write down the same rule," which tells the reader the two match. His shows it and trusts them.

**The piece has to be "from me."** `[article]` The field is named "Insights from me, to you." Every true-corpus piece opens in first person with him inside it: "During the height of covid, I closed the 4th zoom call of the day." "I grew up in the early 2000s." "My deepest secret is on some nights I decide I want to be sad." "As a coach, I see every client as the protagonist in their own story."

The reader recognises themselves by watching him. A draft that runs second person throughout and reaches "I" only in the last line has lost the field's premise, however good the content is.

**Do not invent biography.** Legitimate first-person entries that are not fabricated:
- his relationship to the material ("I keep a chimpanzee study in my coaching notes")
- his coaching observation ("I hear the same 3 stories from almost everyone I coach")
- his witness coda ("I have watched people...")
- the metaphor-I, where the "I" is figuratively rather than literally true

**Recognition, not credit.** `[article]` When he finds his own practice in someone else's work, frame it as surprise, never as having done it first. "I wrote mine before I had ever heard the term" drew: "seems too braggy now. seems like a child arguing I made things first." Quote his worksheet as a moment, never as proof. Using his lines as evidence is what made that draft brag.

**Never "my clients."** `[article]` His words: "'My clients' seems too straightforwardly condescending." Use "the person across from me," "someone I coach," or move to "we."

**"We" is his bridge to the shared human beat**, not "you." His own worksheet reaches for it: "In humans, we sometimes make that mistake at 15."

**Standing gap.** The true corpus scores 7.1 named things per 100 words largely because he is naming things from his own life. A piece built from research instead of memory tops out around 5. He has agreed to supply real details from his own story when they arise. Ask for one. Never manufacture one.

**POV and mode switches need a reasoned, signposted bridge.** His material moves between three registers: third-person story, second-person direct address, and first-person coach narration. Switching is fine and often necessary, but every switch needs two things: a real reason, not associative drift, and an explicit bridge sentence telling the reader the switch is happening. This is tour-guide work. You say something when you turn a corner.

*Diagnosed failure:* a draft jumped from a third-person animal story straight into "You made a version of that mistake once, maybe at 15..." with nothing marking the turn. The raw worksheet had the bridge built in all along ("In humans, we sometimes make that mistake at 15") and the translation dropped it. Losing that sentence is a large part of why the draft read as AI-generated. It substituted an abrupt associative leap for the reasoned pivot a human writer makes on purpose.

*Fix pattern:* a short explicit sentence naming the pivot and giving its reason. Use his own sentence where one exists. "You know that feeling" is too weak, because it asserts the parallel instead of claiming it.

**Corollary, adapting a quote's grammatical person.** When translating a quote across a mode-switch, it can be worth changing its person to match the surrounding register, for example "You should never give up" becoming "I will never give up" inside a first-person passage. Weigh this against whether the quote's power lives in its original wording. If it is a slogan or aphorism whose punch depends on being said exactly that way, keep the original and build the bridge around it instead of into it.

# 5. STRUCTURE AND FLOW

**One arc, told forward.** His diagnostic question, which is the sharpest test in this document:

> "Right now, do you want to tell the story of X getting rejected, or X getting in? I think a big part of confusion is these."

If you cannot answer that in one sentence, the reader is lost. One life, one direction. Raised, cast out, punished, then whatever comes next. Splitting a single arc into "half one, half two" invents a second story that does not exist.

**When his material is a realization, tell it in the order it happened.** `[article]` His brief for Lasting Change Letters #1 said "I realized myself," and every draft that stated the realization early failed. His words: "The main point is about me realizing that I am alike to voss AFTER i read it. maybe as a revelation to the reader," and "this should have been clear from my initial prompt." The flow that worked: distance (a book as far from his work as possible), the idea shown in its own world, the moment he recognises himself, the reveal, then the reader's turn. Hold the realization back until the reader can have it with him.

**When there is a reveal, make sure the reader gets it.** His words: "when we do a big reveal, we make sure the reader gets it." Give the reveal its own paragraph, then say it once more in plain words. When the reveal is a conflict between two sides, give each side its own line. He split "One side wants to try. The other side has a counteroffer" into "One side says... But the other side says..."

**One story, one turn, no return trips.** His pieces run one story and one realization. A draft that went back to the source story for a second visit was flagged as "just slop," with the cause identified as the extra turn. Tell the source story once, all the way through, including the part that will pay off later, then never go back. Setup and payoff, not a second visit.

**Never replace a cut arc with another arc.** His instruction: "let's scrap the getting in. just focus on the rejection I think it's too hard and requires a lot of manual control to get 2 story arcs done right in a small word budget and AI." The next draft went back to the source, found different material, and built a second arc again. **A cut arc leaves a hole that does not want another story in it.** It usually wants stakes (see section 6).

**Glue between beats.** His most frequent note by a wide margin, three times in a single markup: "needs a glue to tie these 2 sentences," "glue to pass the lesson and the narrative." Two adjacent paragraphs that are both good still need a sentence explaining why one follows the other. Never make the reader infer a transition. Confirmed working: "I think it's good to have a glue rule, now sentences flow better."

**The story-to-takeaway glue is a different animal.** His words: "this is the glue between the story and the takeaway. so it must satisfyingly conclude the story." It has two jobs, and the story-conclusion job comes first. A line that abstracts away from the character at that joint will fail, because the reader wants the character's ending at exactly that point. Rejected example: "What he learned at 10 was not wrong. It was just not all of it."

**Let the reader draw the line themselves.** After a story, the line that turns to the reader speaks in the reader's terms and does not repeat the story's image. His words: "it doesn't respect the audiences ability to reason and carry the context of the sentence that just ended... allow the audience to draw the line themselves, hence a more satisfying read."

**A suspense opener is short.** Introducing the idea with suspense works, in few words. His words: "this is too long. the point of introducing idea with suspense is good. but executed with too many words."

**A paragraph he could not have written himself, in his words.** From Lasting Change Letters #1: "Most of us try to win this negotiation by force. More discipline, a better plan, a harder push. But you cannot overpower the other side when the other side is you. Every time you push, it pushes back just as hard, because it is exactly as strong as you are." His verdict: "this is one paragraph here that I would not be able to conjure myself even though that is 100% what I am thinking. and this paragraph is in the right place, with the right development of ideas, was the right bridge between the previous and next paragraph."

What it does: between the diagnosis and the prescription, it names the reader's default fix (force), says plainly why it fails, and extends the piece's own image instead of bringing in a new one. **One confirmed instance. A pattern to reach for, not a required beat.**

**Reference decay.** On "all of it stops" he wrote: "it doesn't 100% refer to the grooming anymore now that it's half sentence ago." A pronoun loses its antecedent faster than you think. Re-name the thing instead of pointing at it.

**Close every loop you open, but never by spoiling the arc.** On "it runs for 10 years" he wrote "(until?)". A stated duration, threshold or condition has to be answered. But answering it by revealing the ending is worse than leaving it open. **Close loops by continuing the timeline**, so the next beat picks the story straight back up and the loop closes itself.

**Never narrate your own structure.** "That is only half of what Reddy watched." "Two lessons, 10 years apart." These explain the shape instead of delivering it. A story told in chronological order needs none of it.

**Presentize the tragedy.** On the paragraph where the cost lands: "(one day, might help here, to presentize the tragedy)". Move from habitual and ongoing into a single specific scene. "One day" does most of the work.

**Show the peril you claim, in a clause.** On "border patrol" he wrote "(needs a clause describing the perils of this)". Adjectives do not count. If you call something dangerous, spend a clause on what the danger actually is.

**A place name needs its category.** He wrote "Ngogo (ngogo what? park? zoo?)". Name what kind of thing it is.

**Every sentence needs an owner.** "That is when the rule gets written" drew "in whose mind?". Agentless and passive constructions hide who is doing the thing, and he catches them every time.

**After every local fix, re-read the whole beat.** Fixing his "(until?)" note by announcing the ending early is what broke the arc in a later draft. A local repair can cause global damage.

**Vary sentence length deliberately.** Include some long winding ones. His own writing breathes, including 40-word sentences: "When everybody went to happy hour to wind down, I would find an empty lecture theatre to sneak in to read Carol Dweck's Mindset, Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow, even foreign books like Ichiro Kishimi's The Courage to be Disliked."

# 6. STAKES AND EMOTION

**When a beat is weak, raise the stakes. Do not add story.** The single biggest lesson from his own rewrite. He deleted three paragraphs of researched narrative and replaced them with two sentences of stakes:

> As an adolescent, this is the biggest threat to his biggest needs: survival, social, and sex.
> So what Melvin has left, is a rule, a regret. To keep reminding him to not repeat the same mistake because the next time he does, he might be outcast for good and that is equivalent to death.

Outcast equals death. That is what makes the mechanism make sense rather than reading as a mood, and it is the emotional engine of the whole piece.

**The AI reflex on a weak beat is to add narrative. His is to raise what is at risk. Adopt his.**

**Raise stakes to what they actually are.** "To survive" beats "at all." *Diagnosed failure:* translating a study's physical stakes ("gets them hurt," "keep him safe") down into mild professional stakes ("one rank below where you've earned to stand") under-escalates. Fix: make the cost concrete and plural. "Whole rooms you didn't speak in, whole asks you didn't make" beats "one rank below."

**Vividness needs an experience the reader already has.** His rule, in his words:

> "a concept that is new by definition cannot be vivid. vivid is only if the audience resonates with the experience, and precise vivid words are used."

Diagnosed on "People who never wrote one keep walking into the same wall." The idea behind it, that people with a weak regret response repeat their mistakes, is new to the reader. Dressing a new idea in imagery produces something that is trying to be vivid and lands as vague. **Explain new ideas plainly. Spend imagery only on experiences the reader has lived.** If a beat cannot be paid for that way, cut it.

**Groups of three signal a swappable category, not a locked list.** "Quit your job, start a blog, pivot to your dream job" is the shape "one dramatic action fixes everything," not three sacred examples. "Getting married will fix everything" is another instance of the same shape. Be liberal, vary the examples.

**Refined (2026-09-02): a triplet works when the three items are different aspects of life. It fails when the three are variations on one aspect.** On "the hand I do not shake, the chair I sit one back from" he wrote: "does not land. I think this is a situation where a group of 3, each on different aspects of life will help." The rhythm was never the problem, the overlap was. The version he called amazing spans romance, work and family:

> The crush who laughed. The interview that said they would call back. The thing you built and carried home, and nobody at the table looked up.

Elsewhere, vary the counts. Repeating the three-item shape at high density is its own tell. Use twos and fours.

**End a list of three on the callback.** The first two items are in the reader's own words, so they see themselves. The third calls back to the story, so the aha lands there. His words (16 Oct 2026): "1 and 2 is audience related, 3 is callback. when audience reads 3, they get the aha." Example: "We should be doing something else." "What about starting out on this new hobby?" "Somewhere out there, there is a better bush."

**A series of ages works as a beat.** "I actually liked the multiples of ages you gave." 25. 35. 45.

**Compression is a payoff, not a standalone move.** "Fear and desire, same address" was flagged as too vague on its own, "might work if in the right place." Short punchlines land after setup, not as freestanding statements.

**Directive tone only works paired with concrete, literal action.** A specific instruction (name your thoughts, give each a percentage) won outright over four directive but vague alternatives. Vague plus directive reads as passive-aggressive, not motivating.

**External pressure is a confirmed, deliberate theme.** Confirmed by him directly (2026-08-31): his writing carries a lot of external pressure elements, other people's expectations, advice, choices made for him, and audiences relate to it. Evidence in the true corpus: the morning radio guru, Robert Kiyosaki, "My parents promised that after studying hard, moving out, and getting paid I will be happy," "My teachers were not impressed." Treat it as a thread worth drawing out, not an incidental detail.

# 7. ENDINGS

**Never end on the hard question alone.** A piece can close on a real, unresolved-feeling question, but it must be followed by a note of genuine, demonstrated possibility, or it reads as diagnostic. The model: "I've witnessed a lot of people turn their lemons into melons."

**The coda must be concrete, and its promise must be liberation, not survival.** "Come out the other side exactly as intact as they hoped" fails twice: abstract, and survival is too low a bar. His own worksheet supplies the right image for Law 2: "a load has been lifted off their chest, for the first time again in their life."

**Insight 3 is the exception.** `[article]` Inside The Introvert's Insight 3 field is explicitly the reader's own unresolved question, and expects no coda, because Insight 1 already carries one for the piece. A deliberate structural exception, not a violation. Confirm with him if a piece's very last visible field is a bare question.

**Insight 3 must open a genuinely new angle**, not restate Insight 1's closing question in different words. His own self-directed worksheet questions are the natural material for it.

**Prescriptive, not philosophical, at the hinge.** He flagged "A lesson you have taken goes quiet. This one is still knocking" with "feels like I am predicting a more prescriptive line. not a philosophical one." At the point where the piece turns, the reader is braced for an instruction, not an aphorism. Aphorisms are texture, not hinges.

# 8. FIDELITY TO HIS MATERIAL

**Argue his thesis, not the genre's.** A draft argued that regret is a bodyguard working an expired shift, so walk through the door anyway. That is "your fear is outdated, be brave," the house style of the entire self-help category, and it is not his. His Law 2 Core Exercise says regret keeps coming back **because it still has a lesson you have not acknowledged.** Not obsolete. Unfinished. His version credits the reader's protective instinct and gives them something to extract, which is what "everyone is resourceful" requires. The genre version tells them to override themselves, which edges into diagnostic.

**The prescription comes from the worksheet, never from invention.** His sharpest structural rejection:

> "everything after this, mainly the diagnosis and prescription, is not what I actually would prescribe. can you refer to worksheet, law 2, and see what my words were"

The rejected payoff fitted well and read well. It was still an invention, and it displaced his method. **Before writing the closing third of any piece, open `build_worksheet.js`, find that Law's Core Exercise, Hidden Reward, self-directed questions and DoD lines, and build the ending out of those. The story is yours to shape. The coaching is not.**

**Finding the material: reason from the inspiration to the worksheet.** `[article]` When a piece starts from something outside his material, a book, a study, a story, its core idea sets the search. Name the core first. Then test each candidate in the worksheet against it: does this item show the core happening, or only sit near it? Take the one that shows it, and prefer a live session over an exercise. In Lasting Change Letters #1 the inspiration was Chris Voss and the core was negotiating with yourself. Nearby material was rejected: an exercise was "much weaker and passive compared to linking it to a session," and a first pick was "not the most useful or adjacent to this story, making it awkward." Ranking by freshness failed too: "how about what relates most to clients negotiating with themselves? cmon." The item that fit was the one that literally was the core, "if I don't try and fail, I'm lazy," a person negotiating with herself. His framing: this is "about how you can reason to find the best fit for current inspiration (voss, negotiating with yourself) with existing material (worksheet)."

**Watch for the structural gift.** His material often contains its own ending. Law 2's third self-directed question, "if I told this as a story about someone else, what would the moral be," is exactly what an article built on an animal study does to the reader, so it closes the piece's own opening line.

# 10. AI SLOP PATTERNS

1. **Unrequested titles.** Never generate a title, subtitle, or framing element that was not asked for. First offense: "The Warning Nobody Updated."
2. **Cliché structural templates, especially in titles.** "The [Noun] Nobody/No One [Verb]" is a generic AI-headline pattern, same family as "X Nobody Talks About" and "The Truth About X." Watch for templated structures anywhere, not just titles.
3. **Metaphor-stacking without cause.** See section 9.
4. **Rhythmic monotony from clipped fragments.** One draft used the same staccato run five times with almost no long sentences anywhere. This happened *while following every sentence-level rule in this document*, so rule compliance is not sufficient.
5. **Repeating the three-item list as a default shape.** Vary the counts.
6. **Over-simplification.** FK 2.4 with no proper nouns is a tell, not a virtue.
7. **The return trip.** Going back to the opening story for a second visit.
8. **Agentless passives.** No owner for the action.
9. **A well-fitting invention in place of his method.** The most dangerous pattern here, because it reads well.
10. **Meta-narration.** Lines that explain the structure instead of delivering it.
11. **Literature-review habits.** Mid-story attribution, hedging inside the narrative, the paper's vocabulary over the reader's.
12. **Adding story where stakes belong.**
13. **Replacing a cut arc with another arc.**
14. **"Stop X. Start Y." bald imperative pairs**, which read as manipulative regardless of content. "Stop performing courage. Start reconnecting with yourself" was rejected as grifter energy, while the same idea inside a fuller sentence was a top pick. The syntax is the trigger, not the content.
15. **The "not just X. Y." scaffold**, when the second half only restates the first, louder. "You're not just off course. You're running the opposite way" was strongly disliked. His own polar prose does not trigger this because each half carries genuinely different concrete content: "If you study and fail, everyone laughs. If you don't study and fail, no one cares." Different scenarios, different outcomes, real information both times. **Test: if the second half could be deleted without losing content, only intensity, that is the tell.**
16. **Naming the powerful word once it has become a genre cliché.** "Why" was flagged as the most powerful of "reason, goal, why" but too close to Sinek's "find your why" to say directly. Ending on a question, or leaving the referent open, gets the payoff without the cliché. **Build toward it, do not name it.**
17. **Cleverness over audience-fit.** Only bust a myth the audience already recognises instantly. A "self-soothe" psychological angle was more original but was rejected: "why inception, busting a myth-bust that isn't shared cultural knowledge yet." Test: would roughly 99% of the target audience recognise this instantly?
