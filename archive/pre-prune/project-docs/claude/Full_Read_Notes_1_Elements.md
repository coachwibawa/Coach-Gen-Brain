# Notes 1: The Elements of Style, Classic Edition (2018 Update)

Read in full, 4 Oct 2026. Source: Mac Book/References. 24,000 words.

## What this edition is (matters for how much weight each part gets)

- **Strunk's own text (1918):** Chapters 1, 2, 4, 5, 6. Rules 1 to 18. This is the core.
- **No E. B. White.** This is not the Strunk and White edition. White's 1959 additions (the "Approach to Style" chapter and his rewrites) are absent. Anything credited to "Strunk & White" from memory that is not in Strunk's 18 rules is not in his copy.
- **The editor, Richard De A'Morelli (2018):** "Editor's Note" boxes, plus Chapters 3 (capitalisation) and 7 (Style Rules for Better Writing), both lifted from his own *Elements of Style 2017*. Modern, practical, lower authority.
- **Foreword by Joseph Devlin:** grammar matters, 9 parts of speech, Orwell's 4 rules and 4 questions.

## Strunk's 18 rules, in his own framing

Strunk's opening condition: "the best writers sometimes disregard the rules of rhetoric. When they do so, however, readers will usually find in the sentence some compensating merit to counterbalance the violation." So the rules are defaults, broken only for a gain the reader can feel.

Rules 1 to 7 (usage): possessive 's, serial comma, commas around parenthetic expressions, comma before a coordinating conjunction, no comma splice, do not break sentences in two, a leading participle must refer to the subject.

Rules 8 to 18 (composition): paragraph as the unit, topic sentence, active voice, positive form, definite specific concrete, omit needless words, avoid a succession of loose sentences, parallel form for coordinate ideas, keep related words together, one tense in summaries, emphatic words at the end.

## The rules that explain his taste-test reactions

### 1. Rule 9 is his "x = y graph"

Strunk takes Stevenson's paragraph on walking tours apart, sentence by sentence:
1. Topic sentence.
2. Meaning made clearer by denying the contrary.
3. Topic sentence repeated in short form, with 3 reasons.
4. A fourth reason, in 2 forms.
5. The same reason in another form.
6 to 7. The same reason in someone else's words (a quote from Hazlitt).
8. The quote repeated in paraphrase.
9. The reason stated a last time, "amplified and heightened to form a strong conclusion."

That is 9 sentences for 1 idea. Every sentence restates, sharpens, proves or illustrates the sentence before it. None of them introduces a new idea. This is the "fractal tree" he praised in Atomic Habits (round 1, passage 14) and the "each sentence... carries their own purpose" he praised in Mindset (passage 1).

Strunk lists the moves a sentence may make on a topic sentence: "restating it in other forms; by defining its terms; by denying the contrary; or by giving illustrations or specific instances... establish it by proofs... develop it by showing its implications and consequences."

**Lesson for my drafts.** Epoch 1 did the opposite: each sentence brought a new idea. That is his "x is not even a function of y". A paragraph should hold 1 idea, and every sentence in it should be one of Strunk's moves on that idea.

### 2. Rule 9 on links between paragraphs

"If the paragraph forms part of a larger composition, its relation to what precedes... may need to be expressed. This can be done sometimes by a mere word or phrase (again; therefore; for the same reason) in the topic sentence." And: "Avoid ending with a digression or an unimportant detail."

**Lesson.** His round 1 note on his own piece: "The first sentence of p2 could be a more connecting or concluding sentence, instead of telling the conclusion." Strunk says the same: the first sentence of a new paragraph carries the link back.

### 3. Rule 4 and Rule 14 explain "then is not the right connector"

Strunk: "the conjunction and is the least specific of connectives. Used between independent clauses, it indicates only that a relation exists between them without defining that relation." His fix: name the relation (as, although, because) so the reader knows whether it is cause, contrast or time.

Rule 14: a run of 2-clause sentences joined by and, but, so, while "soon becomes monotonous", with "mechanical symmetry and sing-song." Fix by recasting "whichever best represent the real relations of the thought."

**Lesson.** "Then she would look at the people around her" used "then" for a relation that was really cause (her plans failed, so she compared). A connector must name the real relation. If it is cause, say so.

### 4. Rule 6 and Rule 13 explain "choppy"

Rule 6: "While it is acceptable to break a compound sentence into two shorter elements... doing so often results in choppy wording." A fragment for emphasis is allowed only when "the emphasis is warranted" ("Again and again he called out. No reply.").

Rule 13, the Macbeth example: "A common violation of conciseness is the presentation of a single complex idea, step by step, in a series of sentences... that might be more effectively combined into one." 6 short sentences (51 words) become 1 sentence (26 words).

Strunk on what concision is NOT: "This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that he make every word tell."

**Lesson.** "A work trip came up, and she was scared it would break her streak. So instead of looking for a gym, she walked everywhere. The streak held." is the Macbeth fault: 1 event cut into steps. One flowing sentence, with the real relation inside it, reads smoother and shorter. This matches his note "short sentences... are used to amplify, not introduce new ideas" (round 1, passage 9).

### 5. Rule 5 is where his colon suggestion comes from

Strunk ranks 3 ways to join 2 related clauses: the semicolon first, because "it suggests a close relationship between the two statements" and is briefer than adding a conjunction. "This simple method of indicating relationship between statements is one of the most useful devices of composition. The relationship... is commonly one of cause or of consequence."

His round 2 notes asked for "a : or some better construction" twice. He was reaching for exactly this device. See warning W1.

### 6. Rule 12 explains "set the stage precisely"

"It is not that every detail is given... but that all the significant details are given, and not vaguely, but with such definiteness that the reader, in imagination, can project himself into the scene." And for ideas: "Even when you are dealing with general principles, you must give instances of their application." Spencer: we "do not think in generals, but in particulars."

**Lesson.** This is both halves of his notes: "this detail is not important" (cut details that are not significant) and "precisely paint the picture" (the significant ones must be definite).

### 7. Rule 18 explains where a line should land

"The proper place in a sentence for the word... that you want to make most prominent is usually the end." This "applies equally to the words of a sentence, to the sentences of a paragraph, and to the paragraphs of a composition."

**Lesson.** End each sentence on its new element, and each paragraph on its point. Several of my sentences ended on a qualifier.

### 8. Rule 15 explains his "forced parallel" note, from the other side

Parallel form is for ideas that truly are coordinate: "The likeness of form enables the reader to recognize more readily the likeness of content and function." The corollary: parallel form on ideas that are not alike tells the reader they are alike when they are not. That is his round 2 note on passage 5: "the parallel is still not that connected or is forced in a context that can't use that structure."

### 9. Rule 11, positive form

"the reader is dissatisfied with being told only what is not; he wishes to be told what is." Use "not" only for denial or antithesis ("Not charity, but simple justice."), never for evasion.

### 10. The "would" of habit (Chapter 5, Would)

"To express habitual or repeated action, the past tense, without would, is usually sufficient, and from its brevity, more emphatic." Example: "Once a year he would visit the old mansion" becomes "Once a year he visited the old mansion."

**Lesson.** My Melina scene ran on "she would write... she would look... she would drop". Plain past tense is firmer, and firmer is the "confident authorship" he keeps asking for.

### 11. Chapter 5, "Interesting"

"Instead of announcing that what you are about to tell is interesting, make it so... Tell the story without preamble." Same for "certainly" and "very": intensifiers weaken.

### 12. Chapter 5, But after but

"Particularly awkward is following one but by another, making a contrast to a contrast or a reservation to a reservation."

### 13. The editor's Chapter 7 confirms his "it" note

"Do not begin sentences with This is because, That is because... Repeat the subject from the previous sentence, even if it requires more words." This is his epoch 0 note: "it. is too vague. repeating the sentence in paraphrase would help here."

Also from Chapter 7 (editor, lower authority): start a sentence with a conjunction sparingly ("choppy or singsong" if overused), paragraphs of 4 or 5 sentences or 100 to 125 words, place long conditions after the main clause, avoid false subjects (it is, there are), "Avoid the pitfall of 'dumbing down' your writing."

### 14. Orwell's 4 questions (Foreword)

"1. What am I trying to say? 2. What words will express it? 3. What image or idiom will make it clearer? 4. Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?" Question 1 asked of every paragraph would have caught most of epoch 1.

## Warnings: where this guide clashes with the Language Rules

Per his decision, these are warnings, case by case. He has also said book craft can override the Language Rules.

- **W1. Semicolons.** Strunk calls the semicolon between related clauses "one of the most useful devices of composition" (Rule 5), and prefers it to "while" (Chapter 5). The Language Rules ban semicolons. His own note asked for "a :" instead. Proposed handling: keep the ban, use the colon where the second clause explains or proves the first, a full stop where the relation is obvious, and a named conjunction (because, although) where it is not. Flag any sentence where none of the 3 works.
- **W2. Numbers.** The editor (Chapter 7) spells out numbers up to 10. Strunk himself only says dates and serial numbers go in figures. The Language Rules say digits. Mindset and Atomic Habits spell out small numbers in running prose ("the third time we met", "two alarms"). Proposed: ask him whether book prose follows the books (words for 1 to 9) or the Language Rules (digits).
- **W3. Paragraph length.** The editor caps paragraphs at 4 or 5 sentences, 100 to 125 words. Strunk's own model paragraph (Stevenson) is 9 sentences. Proposed: follow the idea, not the count. 1 idea per paragraph, as long as the idea needs.
- **W4. "However" first.** Strunk: do not put "However" (meaning nevertheless) first in a sentence. Dweck does, in the Tom and Lucy passage he liked, and his note asked whether "the However holds the key here". Low stakes. Proposed: allow it when it turns the story.
- **W5. Fragments for emphasis.** Strunk allows them only when the emphasis is warranted. The Language Rules have no rule here. No clash, just a limit.

## Questions to read for: what this guide answers

1. Sentence to sentence flow: Rule 9 (every sentence a move on the topic sentence), Rule 4 (name the relation), Rule 14 (no sing-song runs).
2. Beat length: Rule 9's Stevenson paragraph, 9 sentences on 1 idea. Rule 13: concision is "every word tell", not short.
3. Short sentences: Rule 6, only when the emphasis is warranted.
4. Warmth and confidence: Rule 11 (definite assertions), the "would" note (plain past), Rule 15 ("the writer has at least decided on the point").
5. Quoted words: not covered beyond form.
6. Parallels: Rule 15, only for ideas truly alike.
7. Cut or keep: Rule 12 (all significant details, definite), Rule 13 (no needless words, not no detail).
8. Clashes: W1 to W5.
