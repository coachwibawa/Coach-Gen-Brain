## Part I: Principles

### Ch 1, The Transaction: where warmth comes from

"Ultimately the product that any writer has to sell is not the subject being written about, but who he or she is." What holds a reader is "the enthusiasm of the writer for his field. How was he drawn into it? What emotional baggage did he bring along? How did it change his life?"

"This is the personal transaction that's at the heart of good nonfiction writing. Out of it come two of the most important qualities that this book will go in search of: humanity and warmth. Good writing has an aliveness that keeps the reader reading from one paragraph to the next, and it's not a question of gimmicks to 'personalize' the author."

How the chapter is built: a scene (Dr. Brock and Zinsser on a panel), told as alternating answers: "He said it was tremendous fun... I then said that writing wasn't easy and wasn't fun." Then the meaning, then the principle. Scene first, principle last.

**Lesson.** Warmth is the author's own stake on the page: why he cares, what it cost him, what it changed. My drafts reported events without the author's stake in them. That is his "No warmth" (round 1, passage 2) and "still lacking some warmth" (round 2, passage 1).

### Ch 2, Simplicity: the missing link and the wall of context

The list of ways a reader gets lost includes: "Perhaps Sentence B is not a logical sequel to Sentence A; the writer, in whose head the connection is clear, hasn't bothered to provide the missing link."

The test: "what am I trying to say?... have I said it? Is it clear to someone encountering the subject for the first time?"

"If the reader is lost, it's usually because the writer hasn't been careful enough."

The page of his own manuscript, rewritten "four or five times. With each rewrite I try to make what I have written tighter, stronger and more precise."

**Lesson.** This is word for word his round 1 note on passage 4 ("the connection between sentence 1 and 2... Something feels off... it takes energy and it drains readers") and his round 2 BIG note ("they are in a wall of context"). The connection lived in my head, or in the session, and never reached the page. Every client line must pass Zinsser's test: clear to someone meeting it for the first time?

### Ch 3, Clutter: what to cut, and what "cut" does NOT mean

The bracket method: bracket "every component in a piece of writing that wasn't doing useful work... Sometimes my brackets surrounded an entire sentence: the one that essentially repeats what the previous sentence said, or that says something readers don't need to know or can figure out for themselves." "Most first drafts can be cut by 50 percent."

"Is every word doing new work?"

Clutter list: verbs with needless prepositions ("head up", "free up"), "personal", "experiencing", euphemism, jargon, long words for short ones ("assistance" for help, "facilitate" for ease, "implement" for do), and the throat-clearing phrases: "I might add," "It should be pointed out," "It is interesting to note." "If you might add, add it. If it should be pointed out, point it out."

**Tension with his "oversuccinct" complaint, and how it resolves.** Zinsser cuts sentences that repeat without adding. Strunk's Rule 9 keeps sentences that restate the topic in a new form (denial of the contrary, an example, a reason, a quote). The difference is "new work": a restatement that adds a new angle, example or proof does new work. A restatement that adds nothing is clutter. His complaint was never about extra words. It was about missing beats.

### Ch 4, Style: be yourself, relax, have confidence

"style is organic to the person doing the writing... Trying to add style is like adding a toupee." "Readers want the person who is talking to them to sound genuine. Therefore a fundamental rule is: be yourself." This takes 2 things: "They must relax, and they must have confidence."

The stiff opening: "Paragraph 1 is a disaster, a tissue of generalities... But Paragraph 3 begins to have a somewhat human quality, and by Paragraph 4 you begin to sound like yourself." Editors often throw away the first 3 or 4 paragraphs. What an editor hopes to find: "a sentence that says something like 'I'll never forget the day when I...' I think, 'Aha! A person!'"

First person: "Writing is an intimate transaction between two people, conducted on paper, and it will go well to the extent that it retains its humanity. Therefore I urge people to write in the first person."

Hedging kills confidence. Elliot Richardson: "And yet, on balance, affirmative action has, I think, been a qualified success." "A 13-word sentence with five hedging words." "Leaders who bob and weave like aging boxers don't inspire confidence, or deserve it. The same thing is true of writers. Sell yourself... Believe in your own identity and your own opinions."

**Lesson.** This is his "inconfident authorship" (round 1, passage 8) and the confidence he praised in Atomic Habits ("The use of you is confident and decisive"). Confidence on the page is the absence of hedges, plus a writer who states what he thinks.

### Ch 5, The Audience: craft versus attitude

Two separate things. Craft: "there's no excuse for losing readers through sloppy workmanship." Attitude: write for yourself, "be yourself when you write."

"Never say anything in writing that you wouldn't comfortably say in conversation. If you're not a person who says 'indeed' or 'moreover'... please don't write it."

E. B. White's hen essay: "what I like is that this is a man telling me unabashedly about a love affair with poultry that goes back to 1907. It's written with humanity and warmth, and after three paragraphs I know quite a lot about what sort of man this hen-lover is."

**A conversation technique (James Herndon, a teacher and a stubborn pupil):** the whole exchange runs inside 1 narrated paragraph, without quotation marks: "I pointed out that I was talking to him. He indicated he heard me. I inquired then why in hell didn't he sit down. He said he didn't want to. I said I did want him to. He said that didn't matter to him. I said do it anyway. He said why? I said because I said so." Then the narrator steps back and reflects: "Well, that's the way it goes sometimes in schools."

**Lesson for coaching scenes.** A third way to render a conversation, besides Dweck's inline quotes and Clear's back-and-forth lines: report it in the narrator's voice, fast, then step back and say what it meant. Useful for the part of a session that leads up to the moment, so only the key line is quoted.

### Ch 6, Words: why the meaning can be 100% and the feel 60%

Against journalese and clichés: "We know just what to expect. No surprise awaits us in the form of an unusual word, an oblique look."

Sound: "readers... hear what they are reading far more than you realize. Therefore such matters as rhythm and alliteration are vital to every sentence." Paine's "These are the times that try men's souls" against 4 rewrites that mean the same thing: "Paine's phrase is like poetry and the other four are like oatmeal." White "reassembled [a sentence] to end with a phrase that will momentarily linger, or... chose one word over another because he was after a certain emotional weight. It's the difference between, say, 'serene' and 'tranquil'."

"If all your sentences move at the same plodding gait... read them aloud... See if you can gain variety by reversing the order of a sentence, or by substituting a word that has freshness or oddity, or by altering the length of your sentences... An occasional short sentence can carry a tremendous punch. It stays in the reader's ear."

"Writing is learned by imitation... cultivate the best models."

**Lesson.** His round 2 note, "the meaning is 100% there. but better choice of words can take it from 60% to 100% in terms of feel and flow", is this chapter. Meaning is the first draft. Feel is sound, rhythm and emotional weight, and it comes from reading aloud and choosing words for weight, not just accuracy. His decision that "vocabulary is not a concern" frees this up.

### Ch 7, Usage

Good words if they exist. "We turned out to be liberal in accepting new words and phrases, but conservative in grammar." Marianne Moore: "I choose always the grammatical form unless it sounds affected." Avoid jargon verbs ("prioritize", "impact", "input", "ongoing").

## Part II: Methods

### Ch 8, Unity: the cure for "jumbled perspective" and "too many ideas"

"Unity is the anchor of good writing... it satisfies the readers' subconscious need for order and reassures them that all is well at the helm."

3 unities: pronoun (first person participant or third person observer), tense ("choose the tense in which you are principally going to address the reader, no matter how many glances you may take backward or forward"), and mood ("any tone is acceptable. But don't mix two or three").

The Hong Kong travel writer: a real person with his wife Ann, then "suddenly he turns into a travel brochure", then back, then "suddenly the writer is a guidebook... Our writer is gone, and so is Ann, and so, very soon, are we." "Instead of controlling his material, his material is controlling him."

Questions to ask before starting: "In what capacity am I going to address the reader?... What pronoun and tense?... What attitude am I going to take toward the material?... How much do I want to cover?... What one point do I want to make?"

"Every writing project must be reduced before you start to write... think small. Decide what corner of your subject you're going to bite off, and be content to cover it well and stop."

"every successful piece of nonfiction should leave the reader with one provocative thought that he or she didn't have before. Not two thoughts, or five, just one."

"Once you have your unities decided, there's no material you can't work into your frame."

**Lesson.** His round 1 note on passage 8 ("jumbled perspective, jumbled ideas") and his epoch 1 note ("too many ideas being put in (that should be put elsewhere...)") are both unity failures. Epoch 1 changed mood (scene, then lecture, then promise) and carried many points. A section of the book should carry 1 point. The other points go to the chapters where they belong, which also serves his page count.

### Ch 9, The Lead and the Ending: how sentences pull

"The most important sentence in any article is the first one. If it doesn't induce the reader to proceed to the second sentence, your article is dead. And if the second sentence doesn't induce him to continue to the third sentence, it's equally dead. Of such a progression of sentences, each tugging the reader forward until he is hooked, a writer constructs that fateful unit, the 'lead.'"

"Continue to build. Every paragraph should amplify the one that preceded it... take special care with the last sentence of each paragraph, it's the crucial springboard to the next paragraph."

Narrative: "Another approach is to just tell a story... narrative is the oldest and most compelling method of holding someone's attention." Edmund Wilson starts the Dead Sea Scrolls with a boy and a goat: "Wilson doesn't spend any time setting the stage. This is not the 'breakfast-to-bed' format used by inexperienced writers."

Collect more than you use: "Every article is strong in proportion to the surplus of details from which you can choose the few that will serve you best."

Leads to retire: the future archaeologist, the visitor from Mars, the cute event "one day not long ago", the have-in-common lead. "Try to give your lead a freshness of perception or detail."

Endings: "when you're ready to stop, stop." Do not summarise ("In sum, it can be noted that..."): readers "hear the laborious sound of cranking." The best ending "should take your readers slightly by surprise and yet seem exactly right." 2 favourite devices: bring the story "full circle, to strike at the end an echo of a note that was sounded at the beginning", and end on a quotation.

**Lesson.** His "x = y graph" is Zinsser's "each tugging the reader forward". The paragraph's last sentence is the springboard, and the next paragraph's first sentence lands on it. Round 1, his own piece: "The first sentence of p2 could be a more connecting or concluding sentence." The full-circle ending is what he liked when Melina's own words returned at the end of the scene.

### Ch 10, Bits & Pieces: the small tools

- **Verbs:** active, precise. "Did he resign? Did he retire? Did he get fired? Be precise."
- **Adverbs and adjectives:** most are unnecessary. "make your adjectives do work that needs to be done."
- **Little qualifiers:** "a bit," "sort of," "rather," "quite," "very," "pretty much." "Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust. Readers want a writer who believes in himself and in what he is saying... Don't be kind of bold. Be bold."
- **The period:** most writers don't reach it soon enough. Long sentences only if "under control from beginning to end."
- **The semicolon:** "use it with discretion," it slows to "a Victorian pace". He still uses it "usually to add a related thought to the first half of a sentence."
- **The dash:** "invaluable". Use 1: "to amplify or justify in the second part of the sentence a thought you stated in the first part." Use 2: a parenthetical pair. (See warning W6.)
- **The colon:** "bringing your sentence to a brief halt before you plunge into, say, an itemized list."
- **Mood changers:** "Learn to alert the reader as soon as possible to any change in mood from the previous sentence." "But": "there's no stronger word at the start." "Don't start a sentence with 'however', it hangs there like a wet dishrag." Time words ("now," "later," "today," "meanwhile") stop confusion when the time frame shifts. "Always make sure your readers are oriented. Always ask yourself where you left them in the previous sentence."
- **Contractions:** "Your style will be warmer and truer to your personality if you use contractions... when they fit comfortably." Avoid "I'd" (ambiguous).
- **Concept nouns:** sentences with "no people in them" and only "is". "Get people doing things."
- **Overstatement and credibility:** "If the reader catches you in just one bogus statement... everything you write thereafter will be suspect."
- **Paragraphs:** short, but "A succession of tiny paragraphs is as annoying as a paragraph that's too long... they make the reader's job harder by chopping up a natural train of thought." The AP story cut into verbless fragments versus his version built into "a logical unit". "almost all of them think in paragraph units, not in sentence units."
- **"You":** "a godsend to anyone writing an instructional book or a self-help book. The voice of a Dr. Spock... is one of the most reassuring sounds a reader can hear."
- **Rewriting:** "Most rewriting consists of reshaping and tightening and refining... making sure you've given the reader a narrative flow he can follow with no trouble from beginning to end." The newly hatched sentence: "It doesn't lead out of the previous sentence."
- **The annotated rewrite** (the man whose friends deserted him). The editor's bracketed notes on the draft: put "he remembered" first to set the tone. "The contrast supplied by 'however' must come first. Start with 'But.'" "All these sentences are the same length and have the same soporific rhythm; turn this one into a question?" "Sentence essentially repeats previous sentence; kill it or warm it up with specific detail." "Reader is still in the present; reverse the sentence to tell him he's now in the past." "Plant irony early." "Reshape to end with 'most'; the last word is the one that stays in the reader's ear." The rewrite uses questions, a time word, "But", "Nor", and ends on "Or did it?"
- **Othello and Iago:** "The tragic hero of the play is Othello. Small and malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions." Fine alone, wrong in sequence: "The name lingering in the reader's ear is Othello."
- **Trust your material:** feature-style leads cut into snippets ("Whoosh! It was incredible.") hide the story. "color is organic to the fact. Your job is to present the colorful fact." The scout's 4.3 seconds: "As a fact that's self-sufficient. There's no need to add a sentence pointing out that 4.3 seconds is remarkably little time... readers can do their own marveling... The reader plays a major role in the act of writing and must be given room to play it. Don't annoy your readers by over-explaining... Try not to use words like 'surprisingly,' 'predictably' and 'of course,' which put a value on a fact before the reader encounters the fact."

**Lessons from Part II, Ch 10.**
1. His round 2 notes on Dweck ("after a therapy session feels abrupt... Maybe the However holds the key here?") match both guides: Strunk and Zinsser both say not to start a sentence with "However". His ear caught what the guides warn about.
2. "Kill it or warm it up with specific detail" is the exact choice for every sentence that restates.
3. Explaining the context a reader lacks (his "wall of context" note) is not the same as over-explaining the meaning. Give the reader the facts they could not know. Let them draw the meaning.
4. "Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust" is the mechanism behind "confident authorship".

## Warnings so far (case by case, his call)

- **W6. The dash.** Zinsser calls the dash "invaluable" and uses it constantly. The Language Rules ban em dashes. Its first job ("amplify or justify in the second part of the sentence a thought you stated in the first part") is the job his own note gave the colon. Proposed: keep the ban. Use the colon for the amplifying job, commas or brackets for the parenthetical pair.
- **W7. Contractions.** Zinsser: contractions make a style "warmer and truer". Need to check the Language Rules for a contraction rule before deciding.
- **W8. Over-explaining versus the wall of context.** Zinsser warns against telling the reader what they can surmise. His own note asks for more explanation around a client's line. No clash once split: explain the facts the reader lacks, never the meaning.

## Part III: Forms

### Ch 11, Nonfiction as Literature
"The only important distinction is between good writing and bad writing." Nonfiction writers "who come bearing information and who present it with vigor, clarity and humanity."

### Ch 12, The Interview: the chapter that answers the "wall of context"

This is the chapter for writing from his session transcripts.

- **His words beat yours.** "Nothing so animates writing as someone telling what he thinks or what he does, in his own words... They carry the inflection of his speaking voice... As soon as a writer steps in, everyone else's experience becomes secondhand."
- **Distil.** "You'll find that he said much that's not interesting, or not pertinent, or that's repetitive. Single out the sentences that are most important or colorful... Your job is to distill the essence."
- **2 standards: brevity and fair play.** "Your ethical duty to the person being interviewed is to present his position accurately... You might misrepresent him by quoting him out of context, or by choosing only some flashy remark without adding the serious afterthought."
- **Linking across the session is allowed.** "if you find on page 5 of your notes a comment that perfectly amplifies a point on page 2... you will do everyone a favor if you link the two thoughts... This may violate the truth of how the interview actually progressed, but you will be true to the intent of what was said."
- **Cleaning up is allowed, and needed.** "if the speaker's conversation is ragged... the writer has no choice but to clean up the English and provide the missing links."
- **The trap of the felicitous quote (his "wall of context", exactly).** "As you write your article, you type his words exactly as you took them down... Later... you realize that several of the quotes don't quite make sense. When you first heard them they sounded so felicitous that you didn't give them a second thought. Now, on second thought, there's a hole in the language or the logic. To leave the hole is no favor to the reader or the speaker... Often you only need to add one or two clarifying words. Or you might find another quote in your notes that makes the same point clearly. But also remember that you can call the person you interviewed... Don't become the prisoner of your quotes... Never let anything go out into the world that you don't understand."
- **Balance and control.** "try to achieve a balance between what the subject is saying in his words and what you are writing in your words. If you quote a person for three or four consecutive paragraphs it becomes monotonous... You are still the writer, don't relinquish control. But make your appearances useful."
- **Quote first.** "When you use a quotation, start the sentence with it. Don't lead up to it with a vapid phrase saying what the man said... If the man said it, let him say it and get the sentence off to a warm, human start." Break the quote "as soon as you naturally can... but not where the break will destroy the rhythm or the meaning."
- **"He said".** Don't strain for synonyms. "please, please! don't write 'he smiled' or 'he grinned.' I've never heard anybody smile." Use "he explained," "he replied," "he added" only when they carry meaning.
- **Mitchell's Mr. Hunter: compress time, never invent.** Mitchell spliced a year of conversations into one afternoon: "he used a dramatist's prerogative to compress and focus his story... But he never manipulated Mr. Hunter's truth; there has been no 'inferring,' no 'fabricating.' He has played fair." "What's wrong, I believe, is to fabricate quotes or to surmise what someone might have said... When you get people talking, handle what they say as you would handle a valuable gift."

**Lessons.**
1. His quote-first preference (epoch 0, "let's try a construction where this sentence is the first sentence") is Zinsser's rule.
2. The "wall of context" fix has 3 tools: add one or two clarifying words, find another line from the session that says it clearly, or ask him (the "call the person" step). That is the biographer correspondence he set up.
3. Moving the A to J list from earlier in Session 3 next to the realisation is allowed IF it is true to the intent. The transcript says the realisation came from comparing herself with others. So the scene must carry that, not just the list.
4. Translating from Indonesian is a form of cleaning up. Same standard: true to intent, never inferred.

### Ch 13, Writing About Places
- "The mere agglomeration of detail is no free pass to the reader's interest. The detail must be significant."
- Avoid the soft words "that under hard examination mean nothing": "attractive," "charming," "romantic."
- "Your main task... is to find the central idea of the place."
- Pritchett's "his very face sits": "just four short words, but they convey an idea so fanciful that they take us by surprise... With one quick insight Pritchett has caught a whole national trait."
- "The strong emotional content of the book was mainly supplied by what I got other people to say. I didn't need to wax emotional... Beware of waxing."

**Lesson.** A coaching session is a place too: find its central idea and its significant details. Let the client's words carry the emotion. The coach does not wax.

### Ch 14, The Memoir: the author's own story
- "Think narrow... Memoir isn't the summary of a life; it's a window into a life."
- "To write a good memoir you must become the editor of your own life, imposing on an untidy sprawl of half-remembered events a narrative shape and an organizing idea." (This is his own coaching philosophy, the editor, applied to writing.)
- "One secret of the art is detail. Any kind of detail will work... as long as it played a shaping role."
- "The crucial ingredient in memoir is, of course, people... What was it that made them memorable, what turn of mind, what crazy habits?"
- "But the most interesting character in a memoir, we hope, will turn out to be the person who wrote it. What did that man or woman learn from the hills and valleys of life?"
- Kennedy Fraser's opening "seizes our attention with its honesty and vulnerability."
- "Make sure every component in your memoir is doing useful work... see that all the details... are moving your story steadily along."
- "The best gift you have to offer when you write personal history is the gift of yourself."

**Lesson.** His own stories (6 years of drafts, the course he never finished, Atomic Habits and back to zero) are memoir inside the book. They need a narrow window, the shaping detail, and what he learned. Honesty and vulnerability are part of warmth.

### Ch 15, Science and Technology: the x = y mechanism made visible
- "It's just a matter of putting one sentence after another. The 'after,' however, is crucial. Nowhere else must you work so hard to write sentences that form a linear sequence. This is no place for fanciful leaps or implied truths."
- "the reader knows nothing... You can't assume that your readers know what you assume everybody knows."
- "Imagine science writing as an upside-down pyramid. Start at the bottom with the one fact a reader must know before he can learn any more. The second sentence broadens what was stated first... and the third sentence broadens the second."
- How Zinsser reads the chimpanzee article: between every paragraph he writes the reader's question ("But why is it worth page 1?" "But hadn't this been possible before?" "How is it a new step?" "But is it an isolated effort?" "But what is the purpose?"), and the next paragraph answers it. On the Iraq article: "He never forgets where he left his readers in the previous paragraph and what they want to know next."
- Personal handle: Bradbury opens a memory article with the sand thrown in his face as a child. "Use your own experience to connect the reader."
- Roueché "doesn't start with the medical history of poisoning... He gives us a man, and not only a man but a blue one."
- Relate the unfamiliar to the familiar: the vulture's wing, the giraffe's neck, a computer "like a sophisticated pencil".
- Ackerman's 2 bat examples: "how many other examples... did she collect, dozens? hundreds? to be able to choose those two? Always start with too much material. Then give your reader just enough."

**Lesson. This is the most practical tool for his "x = y graph".** Before writing each paragraph, write the reader's question that the last paragraph raised. The paragraph answers it. If no question links them, the order is wrong or a paragraph does not belong. Epoch 1 failed this test.

### Ch 16, Business Writing: the coach's two voices
The Greenwich principals: "Whenever the principals wrote to notify the parents of some human detail, they wrote with humanity... But when the educators wrote to explain how they proposed to do their educating, they vanished without a trace." "The way to warm up any institution is to locate the missing 'I.' Remember: 'I' is the most interesting element in any story." "Readers identify with people, not with abstractions." "vulnerability has a strength of its own."

**Lesson.** My drafts are warm in the scene and cold in the explanation. When the book explains the method, the author must stay a person, not become a framework.

### Ch 17, Sports
Elegant variation: "Bob Hornsby has been metamorphosed within one paragraph into the gangling junior, the Memphis native, the Exeter graduate, the racquet ace and the redhead... Never be afraid to repeat the player's name." "One of them is that you are not the story." Hold the hype: "give us heroes who are believable."

**Lesson.** Keep Melina "Melina". Keep "the pattern" "the pattern". And in a client's scene the client is the hero, the coach is the guide (his own philosophy: the editor, not the hero).

### Ch 18, Writing About the Arts
Use specific detail ("a style that finds portents in lampshades"). Let the author's words document the claim. Opening: "orient your readers to the special world they are about to enter... You can't just throw them in the water and expect them to swim easily. The water needs to be warmed up." Firm opinions: "Don't cancel its strength with last-minute evasions." "let's not go peeing down both legs."

**Lesson.** A coaching session is a "special world". Warm the water before the client's key line.

### Ch 19, Humor
"Humor is built on surprise." Cut "the extra sentence that explains a funny point that is already implicit." "start from the truth rather than from invention." The narrator as "the victim or dunce" lets readers identify. Benchley "added a dimension of warmth and vulnerability."

## Part IV: Attitudes

### Ch 20, The Sound of Your Voice
- "Don't alter your voice to fit your subject. Develop one voice that readers will recognize."
- The effortless style "is achieved by strenuous effort... The grammar is formal, the words are plain and precise, and the cadences are those of a poet... The writer sounds confident; he's not trying to ingratiate himself with the reader."
- Against breeziness: "I stop reading writers who say 'You see.'" "readers will stop reading you if they think you are talking down to them."
- Taste: "knowing what not to do is a major component of taste." "Clichés are the enemy of taste." Taste is "an ear that can hear the difference between a sentence that limps and a sentence that lilts."
- "After verbs, plain nouns are your strongest tools; they resonate with emotion."
- Eloquence "moves us with what it leaves unsaid, touching off echoes in what we already know."
- "Never hesitate to imitate another writer."

### Ch 21, Enjoyment, Fear and Confidence
- Perelman: "The reader has to feel that the writer is feeling good." "Even if he isn't."
- "If you want your writing to convey enjoyment, write about people you respect."
- Sincerity is a credential: "Your best credential is yourself."
- Interviewing an expert: "is the expert's first answer sufficient? Usually it's not." Keep asking "Why not?" and "What else?". "Have I seen everything?": "Often you'll get your best material after you put your pencil away."
- "Trust your curiosity to connect with the curiosity of your readers."

**Lesson.** My biographer questions to him should follow the Peterson method: never take the first answer, ask why and what else.

### Ch 22, The Tyranny of the Final Product
- Compression: "nobody can write an article about the disappearance of small towns in Iowa... The writer would have to write about one small town... to one store, or one family, or one farmer."
- Focus: "Of all the possible stories that you might distill out of this place, which one is your story?"
- Voice: "Readers won't put up with vacillating between two perspectives and tones."
- The quest: "any time you can tell a story in the form of a quest or a pilgrimage you'll be ahead of the game. Readers bearing their own associations will do some of your work for you."
- Intention: "We can write to affirm and to celebrate, or we can write to debunk and to destroy... Writing is related to character."

**Lesson.** The book's reader is on a quest: to finish something. Every story in it can be told as a quest. And intention to affirm matches his core philosophy (empowering, never pitying).

### Ch 23, A Writer's Decisions: "The News From Timbuktu", annotated
The whole case for the x = y graph in one passage: "writing is linear and sequential... logic is the glue that holds it together... tension must be maintained from one sentence to the next and from one paragraph to the next and from one section to the next, and... narrative, good old-fashioned storytelling, is what should pull your readers along without their noticing the tug... Every step should seem inevitable."

- "Each sentence contains one thought, and only one. Readers can process only one idea at a time, and they do it in linear sequence."
- "The first sentence of that paragraph grows out of the last sentence of the previous paragraph; the reader is given no chance to squirm away."
- The second paragraph "acknowledges what the reader already knows, or half knows... It thereby welcomes him as a fellow traveler." (His round 1 praise: "checking in on the audience with questions.")
- "Now, what do your readers want to know next? Ask yourself that question after every sentence."
- "you must take them on your trip. Make them identify with you, with your hopes and apprehensions."
