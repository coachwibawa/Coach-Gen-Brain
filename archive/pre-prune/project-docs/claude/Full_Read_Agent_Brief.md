# Reading brief: what makes Coach Gen like these books

You are reading one of 3 reference books for Coach Gen (Genison Wibawa), an ICF-certified coach writing his first book, "5 Laws of Lasting Change" (subtitle: "If you keep trying, but never finish, this is why."). Claude is ghostwriting it, biographer-style, from his real material. His verdict on Claude's book drafts so far: about 40% of a book. The goal of this read is to find out exactly what these authors do that he likes, so it can be reproduced. Accuracy matters more than volume.

## What he said about the 3 books (from memory, not read in a year)
- Atomic Habits (James Clear): "I like for presenting and layering of ideas. confidence and convincing authoring that makes reader trust the book."
- Mindset (Carol Dweck): "I like for the relatability, presentation, structure of case studies."
- The Courage to Be Disliked (Kishimi and Koga, English translation): "I like for conversation and structure of book, I purposely gave you the english version. has some obvious translation cruxes, but still reads well."
- Point of view chosen for his book: Dweck and Clear blend. The author "I" tells other people's stories and his own, and speaks to the reader as "you". He wants to open the book like Dweck's Introduction and Chapter 1.

## Questions to read for (answer each, with evidence)
1. Sentence to sentence: what makes one sentence lead into the next? Name the actual devices (repeated key word, pronoun, question-then-answer, "But", time words, cause words). Count roughly how paragraphs begin.
2. Paragraph to paragraph: how does each paragraph link to the one before? Is there a reader's question in between? Show 3 to 5 worked examples (quote the last sentence of one paragraph and the first sentence of the next, with line numbers).
3. One idea per section: how long does the author stay on one idea before moving on? How many paragraphs? How is a NEW concept introduced and explored before the next one arrives?
4. Examples: how many per idea, what shape, how varied (different areas of life)? How do lists of examples stay parallel?
5. Case studies and people: how is a person introduced (name, age, job, situation)? How much context before their quoted words? How long is the case? How does the author come back to it? How are quotes made to make sense to a reader who wasn't there?
6. The author's own stories: where do they appear, how long, how vulnerable, how they connect to the point (mirroring)?
7. Warmth and confident authorship: concrete devices. Hedges or none? How strongly are claims stated? How does the author check in with the reader ("you", questions)?
8. Conversation: how are 2 to 3 exchanges rendered (inline in a paragraph, separate lines, reported speech)? What carries the narration between lines?
9. Metaphors and analogies: what kind (familiar, everyday)? How often? How long is one held?
10. Openings and endings of chapters and sections. Chapter structure overall (story first? summary boxes? exercises?).
11. Short sentences: where, how often, what job?
12. Mechanics, measured with Python on the text: average words per sentence, share of sentences under 8 words, average paragraph length in words and sentences, contractions per 1000 words, small numbers as words vs digits, em dashes and semicolons per 1000 words, how many paragraphs open with a quote. (These matter because his Language Rules ban em dashes and semicolons and write numbers as digits.)
13. Where the book breaks the writing-guide rules (Strunk and Zinsser) and it still works. Say why it works.

## How to work
- The book is a plain text file. Read ALL of it with the Read tool in chunks (offset and limit, about 600 to 900 lines at a time). Do not skim with grep. Use grep only to find chapter boundaries first.
- Take notes as you go and append them to your notes file after every chunk or two, so nothing is lost if you are interrupted. Use the notes file path given in your task.
- Every claim needs evidence: a short exact quote (under 40 words) with its line number in the text file. For longer model passages, give the line range instead of copying.
- Be concrete. "Uses vivid examples" is useless. "Opens each of 3 paragraphs with the same 3-word frame, then varies the second half (lines 412 to 430)" is useful.
- Do not invent. If something is not in the text, do not claim it.
- No em dashes and no semicolons in your own notes (house style).

## Output (in your notes file)
1. Book map: chapters with line ranges, and the shape of a typical chapter.

3. Answers to the 13 questions above, each with evidence.

6. "What not to copy": anything that clashes with his style or Language Rules, or that he reacted against.
