# Findings from the 3 books (4 Oct 2026)

Full read of Atomic Habits (Clear), Mindset (Dweck) and The Courage to Be Disliked (Kishimi and Koga), each read in full by a reading agent with one brief (claude/Full_Read_Agent_Brief.md). Every quote in the notes carries a line number in the plain text files. The agents checked them by script, and Claude spot-checked samples against the source. Full notes: claude/Full_Read_Notes_3_Atomic_Habits.md, claude/Full_Read_Notes_4_Mindset.md, claude/Full_Read_Notes_5_Courage.md.

These are findings, not rules. A finding that the 2 guides and all 3 books agree on is marked **[5 of 5]**. These are the strongest candidates for Book Rules v2.

## A. Why round 3 failed, seen through the books

- **"robotic and repeated."** Clear breaks every run of 3 or 4 same-shape sentences with one long sentence that moves the idea on (the 41-word sentence after the "lagging measure" run, AH 529 to 531). Courage's test for a restatement: delete it. If nothing is lost but volume, it was a repeat, not a step. Round 3 had parallel runs with no break, and restatements that added nothing.
- **"too many differing concepts that were unexplored."** Clear stays on one idea for 8 to 25 paragraphs per heading (show it, name it, say it plainly, apply it, prove it, compress it). Dweck tells a case inside one timeframe and ends on a word planted before it. Round 3 put 3 concepts in its last 3 sentences.
- **"vague metaphor no one ever uses."** All 3 books take images from the reader's own memory, the author's own life, the client's own words, or a story everyone knows. Courage starts most analogies from "have you ever..." or names the picture already in the reader's head, then corrects it. "Never left the page" came from none of these.
- **"wtf is this?"** Clear never names a concept before the reader has seen it. "What finally worked for me was slower" named a thing the reader had not seen.
- **"Healthy."** None of the 3 books sources a person's private detail without a reason in the scene. Dweck's rule for background: label it, keep it to one paragraph of facts, then the story (Mindset 7257 to 7263).

## B. The moves all 5 sources share

1. **The reader's question sits between paragraphs, and the next first sentence answers it. [5 of 5]** Dweck prints the question and answers it at once ("What did they know? They knew...", MS 137 to 138), 129 times in the book. Clear writes the reader's doubt into the text ("You may be thinking...", "Why is this important?"). In Courage, the youth is the reader's voice. Zinsser does the same when he reads a science article.
2. **Each paragraph hands a word to the next. [5 of 5]** Clear ends on a key word and starts the next paragraph with it ("slide", AH 487 to 489). Dweck picks up a word and narrows it ("our work" to "My work", MS 50 and 53). Measured in Clear: 63% of adjacent sentences share a visible link (repeated word 38%, connector 15%, pronoun 15%, same opening frame 11%). Plain connectors are rare ("However" opens only 12 sentences in the whole book).
3. **One idea per section, explored before the next arrives. [5 of 5]** Clear: show, name, restate plainly ("That is,"), apply in 3 everyday examples, prove, compress into one line, then a question hands off to the next heading.
4. **Show it before naming it. [4 of 5]** Clear never names a concept before a scene. Dweck's word "mindset" first appears in quote marks, as the name for what she has already described (MS 49 to 67). Courage names a term after the experience ("Name the term after the experience", CG 2445 to 2447).
5. **Examples: one frame, varied slots, and the items truly alike. [5 of 5]** Clear's "Your X is a lagging measure of your Y habits" with 4 slots from different areas of life, then "You get what you repeat." (AH 523 to 527). Strunk: parallel form only for truly matching ideas. Your note: "the and you should be parallel."
6. **Short sentences have a job. [5 of 5]** They compress, turn, reveal, answer, or hold a beat ("Ten seconds passed.", AH 112). They never bring a new fact. Clear 13.8% of sentences are under 8 words, Dweck 22.5%.
7. **Confidence is calibrated, not loud. [5 of 5]** Flat claims where the evidence is solid ("It was mindset.", MS 3334). A hedge only at a real limit ("Can anyone do anything? I don't really know.", MS 2151). Clear: 2 soft hedges per 1000 words, a first-person verdict ("I think you would."), and one honest limit per chapter. Courage: 2-word answers to the biggest claims ("Yes, I am. Adamantly.").
8. **Warmth is the author's own story, short, mirrored and admitting something. [5 of 5]** Clear: 1 to 8 lines from his own life, right after a case, with the same shape and a small failing. Dweck: vulnerable stories ("So I cried."). Courage: the philosopher applies the hard claim to himself first, with vulnerability rising across the book. All 3 take the blame off the reader ("the problem isn't you. The problem is your system.", AH 770 to 771).
9. **A case study has a set shape. [4 of 5]** One sentence gives the person (name, role, what is at stake). The situation and stakes come before they speak. An action or feeling comes right before a short quote. The quote is long enough to carry itself, or comes last as the punch. Then one sentence gives the meaning. Models: Jim Marshall (MS 1046 to 1063), Brian Clark (AH 910 to 931), the blushing student (CG 1149 to 1158).
10. **The mirror needs matching beats. [4 of 5]** Dweck: the case, a 1 or 2 sentence idea between, an explicit link ("I, too...", "I almost fell into the same trap."), then her own story with the same beats from the opposite angle, at similar length (Robert, MS 1019 to 1035). If the beats do not match, there is no mirror.
11. **2 or 3 exchanges of conversation, in prose. [4 of 5]** The 3 models agree.
    - Clear (the weightlifting coach, AH 5699 to 5706): his question quoted, the first answer reported, the real answer quoted after a colon, then his reaction.
    - Dweck: inside the paragraph. Quote the turns that carry feeling, report the turns that carry facts, and put something human between or after them (a look, "I'm glad I asked.").
    - Courage (CG 1188 to 1197): plain verbs that carry intent (asked, went on, pressed), the client's lines short, the coach's lines carrying the turn, and a time word between beats ("Then one day").
    - One narrated beat can hold a silence: "Ten seconds passed." (your note: "should be fine to use as coaching slow paced too").
12. **A surprising idea never arrives bare. [Courage, with Clear and Dweck in support]** One of 4 things sits right beside it: an example the reader has lived, the reason in the next sentence, premises the reader already accepted, or the reader getting there first. Your note on passage 17 ("presented directly, which can be hard to swallow") matched the one place the book breaks this.
13. **Plant and pay off threads. [all 3 books]** A word, image, case or promise appears early, comes back in the middle with something new, and pays off near the end. The callback uses the exact words, with a reminder tag ("Jimmy, the hard-core turned-off student from chapter 3", MS 7130). This is the "structure of book" you remembered liking in Courage, and it is what makes a book read as one line.
14. **Endings: the relationship, the echo, or one sayable line. Never a teacher's summary. [5 of 5]** Courage ends every night on the relationship. Clear bookends chapters with the opening story. Dweck closes a case on an image ("One year later, he put a green jacket on Mark O'Meara.", MS 3143 to 3144).
15. **Openings. [Dweck model]** The Introduction (MS 49 to 78): a 1-sentence origin scene with a person in it, then her authority, then the promise to "you". The title word arrives as a name in quote marks, followed by a flat claim and 3 promises that narrow from public to private, then the anonymity note in brackets. Chapter 1 (MS 117 to 132): her own scene in time order, one job per sentence, 139 words before the first quoted line, and one sensory sentence ("grunted, perspired, and toiled") that sets up the surprise.

## C. Narration, the part of Courage you liked

It is only 3% of the book (20 passages of about 88 words), and every passage sits at a turning point. Each one mixes the body, the setting, the inner voice and a confident chain of cause and effect, all in one passage (CG 254 to 262). Setting appears only when it carries feeling. For 5 Laws, this is the model for a coaching scene's setup paragraph.

- **Em dashes and semicolons:** every one in the 3 books can become a comma, colon, brackets or a full stop. 5 of the 6 Atomic Habits passages you loved have no em dash. The ban costs little.
- **W2, numbers (still yours to decide):** all 3 books write small counts as words ("Ten seconds passed") and use digits for measures, percentages and data. Your Language Rules say digits everywhere.
- **W7, contractions (still yours to decide):** all 3 books use them, and they mark the warm, spoken voice. Your published pieces use none.

## E. What not to copy

Em dashes and semicolons. Clichés and American idioms ("fight tooth and nail", "face the music"). Comma splices. Judging labels ("losers", "addicts", "a fixed mindsetter", "pitying himself"), because the project rule is never diagnostic and never pitying. Mocking a person ("Hi there, John."). Script-format dialogue and filler turns ("Yes.", "I see."). Background dumped as a lecture. The translation's "one" and jargon ("teleology"). Parades of famous names: copy the structure of a case, not the sourcing. Housekeeping inside the opening (thanks, edition notes).

## F. After Chapter 1 epoch 1 (5 Oct): research, quotes, headings, how many terms

His words: "still feels like a 70, need to get to 95 or 99 so I can finetune well." Then 5 notes: research "very jarring", Insight 2 and 3 optional, quotes inline, subheadings "too robotic", and "keep down the number of vocabulary the reader needs to keep in his head." Measured by script on the plain texts (AH lines 77 to 6520, MS lines 100 to 8200).

**1. How they bring in other people's research.**
- **No years, no institutions, few names.** Dweck: 148 sentences about research, 0 with a year. Her own praise study, the same one Chapter 1 epoch 1 used, is told with no year and no co-author: "So we conducted studies with hundreds of students, mostly early adolescents." (MS 2174 to 2175). Clear gives a year only when a study is the opening story of a chapter (Thorndike 1898, the 248 exercisers 2001, Olds and Milner 1954, Lewin 1936: 4 or 5 times in the book). Mid-chapter it is "In one study, scientists..." (AH, the insomnia and dopamine studies), "Researchers have even found that..." (AH 1829), "one study found that..." Institutions appear 4 times in AH and 8 in MS, almost all as part of a person's life, not as a citation.
- **A name only with a role tag, and only when the person matters.** "Stanford professor BJ Fogg" (AH), "Howard Gardner, in his book Extraordinary Minds, concluded that..." (MS 380), "Laura Kray and Michael Haselhuhn have shown that..." (MS 4276). One name, a role, a short inline quote or one finding. Never 2 names plus a year plus a university.
- **The idea comes first, the study backs it.** "The good news? You can train yourself to link a particular habit with a particular context. In one study, scientists..." (AH). Dweck opens with the reader's belief ("more than 80 percent of parents told us it was necessary to praise children's ability", MS 2158), then her worry, then "What's great about research is that you can ask these kinds of questions and then go get the answers." (MS 2173 to 2174).
- **2 to 5 sentences, one finding, then the meaning in plain words.** "The punch line is clear: people who make a specific plan for when and where they will perform a new habit are more likely to follow through." (AH 1836 to 1837). "What's so alarming is that we took ordinary children and made them into liars, simply by telling them they were smart." (MS 2247 to 2248).
- **Research never interrupts the author's own story.** Clear's own story (Introduction, about 2,000 words over 3 sections) holds no study at all. Science arrives only after it, in "HOW THIS BOOK WILL BENEFIT YOU". Dweck's Chapter 1 story holds research only because she was the researcher. Chapter 1 epoch 1 dropped 3 studies into his story, each with 2 names, a year and, twice, a university. That is the jarring part: each one stops his life and opens a lab.

**2. Quotes inline.**
- Paragraphs that are only a quote: AH 13 of 1,130 (1%), MS 33 of 1,327 (2.5%). Paragraphs with a quote inside the prose: AH 192, MS 449.
- A quote stands alone only in 2 cases: a quick exchange of dialogue in a scene ("What year is it?", AH 106), or a set of sample sentences shown as a list (Clear's "I'm terrible with directions." / "I'm not a morning person.", Dweck's survey answers).
- Self-talk sits inside the sentence: "What's wrong with them? I wondered." (MS 130). "We tell ourselves, "I'm going to eat healthier" or "I'm going to write more," but we never say when and where" (AH 1839 to 1840).
- The author's own payoff line can stand alone as its own short paragraph, without quote marks: "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." (AH 774 to 775).
- Chapter 1 epoch 1 set 8 quotes on their own lines, including the 2 praise sentences and the 2 sentences repeated as a pair.

**3. Headings.**
- Both books use headings, about one per 400 to 600 words: MS Chapter 1, 3,800 words, 6 headings. AH Chapter 1, 4,300 words, 6. AH Introduction, 3,000 words, 4.
- **The opening story never has one.** MS Chapter 1 runs 428 words before the first heading, AH Chapter 1 666 words, AH Introduction 830 words.
- **A heading names the next idea, or asks the reader's question.** "WHY DO PEOPLE DIFFER?", "SO, WHAT'S NEW?", "WHAT'S IN STORE" (MS). "WHY SMALL HABITS MAKE A BIG DIFFERENCE", "WHAT PROGRESS IS REALLY LIKE", "FORGET ABOUT GOALS, FOCUS ON SYSTEMS INSTEAD" (AH). The section before ends on a question, and the heading answers it: Brailsford's story ends "How does this happen?", and the next heading is "WHY SMALL HABITS MAKE A BIG DIFFERENCE".
- **When the author's own story is headed, the heading is a plain label:** "MY RECOVERY", "HOW I LEARNED ABOUT HABITS" (AH Introduction). Never a line lifted from the story.
- Chapter 1 epoch 1 used 4 headings, each the title of an article act or a line from the story ("I Did Change", "30 Days of Doing Nothing"). They cut one continuous thread into acts, and the same names came back as the group names in the From Within You box. That is the robotic part, not the number.

**4. How many terms the reader holds.**
- Dweck's Chapter 1 asks the reader to hold 2 named things: the fixed mindset (11 times) and the growth mindset (18 times). Each idea keeps one phrase, repeated word for word: "carved in stone" (4 times) against "cultivated" (4 times). Many famous names pass through (Binet, Gottlieb, Sternberg, Darwin, Tolstoy, Ben Hogan), each used once with a role tag and then dropped. The reader never has to remember them.
- Clear's Chapter 1 names 3 things, each shown before it is named: compounding (1 percent better), the Plateau of Latent Potential, and systems over goals.
- Chapter 1 epoch 1 asked the reader to hold: the bar, talent, 30-day challenges, 30 days of doing nothing, mantra, head start, announcing, the false hope syndrome, "the pattern", three acts (and three groups in the box, and three stages in the plan), "my two sentences", the turning point, easy rewards, Lasting Change (and "lasting change" in the locked line), the 5 Laws, 5 Law names, and 5 domain words (direction, past, present, future, results). Plus 7 people's names (Claudia Mueller, Carol Dweck, Janet Polivy, Peter Herman, Peter Gollwitzer, Max Hertan, Michael) and 2 universities.
- The drift is the other half: one thing with 3 names (acts, groups, stages), "bar" swapping with "goal" and "challenge", "comfortable" with "comfort" and "easy reward".
