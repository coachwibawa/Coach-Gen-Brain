# Findings from the 2 writing guides (4 Oct 2026)

Distilled from the full read of The Elements of Style (2018 Classic Edition) and On Writing Well (6th edition). Full notes: claude/Full_Read_Notes_1_Elements.md and claude/Full_Read_Notes_2_Zinsser.md.

These are findings, not rules yet. The rules get built after the 3 books are read too. Each finding names the note of his it explains.

**First, what his copy of Strunk is.** It is Strunk's 1918 text plus a 2018 editor's notes and 2 new chapters. E. B. White is not in it. So "Strunk and White" in the project instructions means, for this book, Strunk's 18 rules.

## The 16 findings

1. **One idea per paragraph. Every sentence makes a move on it.** Strunk's Rule 9 takes a paragraph apart: topic sentence, the contrary denied, the topic restated with reasons, the same reason in other words, a quote, the quote paraphrased, a heightened close. 9 sentences, 1 idea. Explains: "x = y graph", "fractal tree", "too many ideas", "x is not even a function of y".
2. **The reader's next question links the paragraphs.** Zinsser reads a good article by writing the reader's question between paragraphs ("But hadn't this been possible before?"). Each paragraph answers the question the last one raised. "Ask yourself that question after every sentence." The last sentence of a paragraph is the springboard. The first sentence of the next grows out of it. Explains: "x = y graph", round 1 passage 4 ("the connection between sentence 1 and 2").
3. **Name the real relation between 2 clauses.** "And" is "the least specific of connectives" (Strunk). Say "because" for cause, "but" for contrast, a time word when time shifts. Explains: "then is not the right connector".
4. **One event is one sentence.** Cutting a single idea into steps is a fault (Strunk's Macbeth example: 51 words in 6 sentences become 26 in 1). Zinsser's AP story, cut into verbless pieces, "chopping up a natural train of thought". A short sentence is for emphasis that is earned. Explains: "choppy", and his round 1 note that short sentences "amplify, not introduce new ideas".
5. **The colon does the joining job.** Strunk prefers the semicolon for 2 closely linked statements. Zinsser uses the dash to "amplify or justify" the first half. Both are banned here. The colon does the same job. Explains: his 2 requests for "a :".
6. **Concise means every word does work, not short.** Strunk: concision "requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail... but that he make every word tell." A sentence that only repeats the last one: "kill it or warm it up with specific detail" (Zinsser). Explains: "oversuccinct" on one side, "ideas repeated" on the other.
7. **Every significant detail, made definite. Drop the rest.** Strunk: "all the significant details are given, and not vaguely." Zinsser: "The detail must be significant." Explains: "this detail is not important" and "precisely paint the picture".
8. **Warm the water before a client's line.** A line can sound perfect in the session and leave "a hole in the language or the logic" on the page (Zinsser). The fixes: add 1 or 2 clarifying words, find another line from the session that says it clearly, or ask the person. Compress time fairly, never invent words. Explain the facts the reader lacks, never the meaning. Explains: the BIG "wall of context" note, and "Maybe I just can't" being too short.
9. **Quote first. Let the person speak.** "When you use a quotation, start the sentence with it" (Zinsser). The client's words carry the emotion. The writer does not "wax". In her scene the client is the hero and the coach is the guide. Explains: his "construction where this sentence is the first sentence", and the "great coach, warm interaction" note.
10. **Warmth is the author's stake, shown.** "the product that any writer has to sell is not the subject... but who he or she is" (Zinsser). Why the author cares, what it cost him, honesty and vulnerability. A person in the explanation too, not only in the scene: Zinsser's school principals were warm about traffic and cold about teaching. Explains: "No warmth" and "still lacking some warmth".
11. **Confidence is a writer who has decided.** No little qualifiers ("Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust"). Positive form (Strunk 11). Plain past tense, not "she would" (Strunk). Firm opinions. Explains: "inconfident authorship", and his praise of Atomic Habits as "confident and decisive".
12. **Parallel form only for ideas that truly match.** "The likeness of form enables the reader to recognize more readily the likeness of content" (Strunk 15). Matching form on unmatched content claims a likeness that is not there. Explains: "the parallel is... forced", "not enough material".
13. **One pronoun, one tense, one mood, one point per section.** "don't mix two or three" (Zinsser on unity). "Every successful piece of nonfiction should leave the reader with one provocative thought... Not two thoughts, or five." Explains: "jumbled perspective, jumbled ideas".
14. **Feel comes from sound and weight.** Read it aloud. Choose words for their emotional weight ("serene" or "tranquil"). End a sentence on its strongest word (Strunk 18). Zinsser spent an hour on one sentence until "fifties and sixties" became "from late middle age to Medicare". Explains: "the meaning is 100% there... from 60% to 100% in terms of feel and flow".
15. **Check in with the reader.** Acknowledge what the reader already half knows and "welcome him as a fellow traveler" (Zinsser on Timbuktu). Tell it as a quest: readers bring "their own associations". Explains: his praise of Mindset for "checking in on the audience with questions".
16. **End by echoing the start, then stop.** No summary, readers "hear the laborious sound of cranking." The full-circle ending "completing with its resonance the journey". Explains: why the return of Melina's own words worked for him.

## Warnings: where the guides and the Language Rules disagree

He decides case by case. Book craft may override the Language Rules (his decision, 4 Oct).

- **W1. Semicolons.** Strunk calls the semicolon "one of the most useful devices of composition". Language Rules: banned. Proposal: keep the ban, use the colon (finding 5). No decision needed unless a sentence cannot work any other way.
- **W2. Numbers.** The Strunk editor, Mindset and Atomic Habits write small numbers as words in prose ("the third time", "two alarms"). Language Rules: digits, and "Spelled-out numbers are an instant tell." **Needs his decision for the book.**
- **W3. Paragraph length.** The Strunk editor: 4 or 5 sentences. Strunk's own model: 9. Proposal: as long as the one idea needs.
- **W4. "However" first.** Both guides say no. Dweck does it in the passage he liked, and he asked if it "holds the key". Proposal: allow it when it turns the story.
- **W6. The dash.** Zinsser calls it "invaluable". Language Rules: banned. Proposal: keep the ban, the colon takes its first job, commas or brackets the second.
- **W7. Contractions.** Zinsser: they make a style "warmer and truer". His 2 published pieces in the taste test use none. Mindset and Atomic Habits use them in moderation. **Tested lightly in round 3. Needs his decision.**
- **W8. Over-explaining.** Zinsser warns against telling readers what they can surmise. His note asks for more explanation around a client's line. Resolved by finding 8: explain the facts, not the meaning.
- **Vocabulary.** The Language Rules' second-language audience note is relaxed for the book by his decision. Zinsser's bar still holds: words "vivid and precise, but not long or fancy".
