# Rhyme this

His words (30 Oct 2026), on "before we farmed corn and raised cattle, we were gatherers": "any way to make this rhyme or sound nice in any way? maybe this can be made into a skill or command; like /rhyme-this, but of course, it's not just about rhyme."
And (3 Nov 2026): "yes, I want the corn and cattle to rhyme too, seems too perfect. I allow the objects to change too".

The job: give him a few versions of one line that sound good when read aloud, so he can pick. Sound is the goal. Rhyme is one way to get there, not the only one.

## Where the request comes from

1. **In chat:** /rhyme-this plus a line, or a pointer to a line in a draft.
2. **In a Google Doc comment:** a comment that contains /rhyme-this. Nothing runs when he types it in Docs. It is picked up the next time the doc is read with comments, in any markup round. The line to work on is the text the comment sits on. If the comment also holds his own wording in [square brackets], start from his wording (Book Rules section 0: [] means up to about half may be rewritten, keeping his essence).

Read the paragraph around the line before writing anything. A line that sounds good alone can break the sentence before it or after it.

## 1. Pin down what must survive

- **The fact.** What the line says that is true. This never changes.
- **Its job.** Setup, turn, payoff, or hand-off. A payoff can carry a strong sound. A setup should not draw more attention than the payoff it sets up.
- **The link.** The word the next sentence picks up (Book Rules R6). Keep it, or move it to where the next sentence can still take it.
- **The objects.** Change the things named in the line only if he allows it for this line, and only to things just as true and just as concrete. On 3 Nov he allowed it for the corn and cattle line.

## 2. Pick the sound devices that fit

Try several. Each option should rest on a different one.

- **Rhyme in a pair:** two named things that rhyme (plough and cow, farms and barns, goats and oats).
- **Near rhyme or a shared vowel:** wheat and sheep.
- **Alliteration:** "walked to, not waited for".
- **Contrast with matching shape:** two halves built the same, with different content in each (Language Rules section 10, item 15: no half that only repeats the first, louder).
- **Rhythm:** a long sentence that runs, then a short one that lands. Or three beats that rise.
- **Echo:** the line repeats words the reader met earlier, so it sounds like a callback (Book Rules R25).

## 3. Write the options

- Three to five, numbered, each labelled with its device in a few words.
- Keep the words his clients know. Many read English as a second language. A rhyme that needs a rare word fails.
- No made-up image, no cliché, no em dash, no semicolon, British spelling.
- The reader must get it on the first read. A line that has to be read twice (a garden-path sentence) fails, however good it sounds.
- Not too cute for its place. A rhyme in the middle of an argument can sound like a jingle.
- Say which one you would pick and why, in one line.

## 4. Deliver

- In chat: the numbered options, nothing else.
- In a markup round: the options go in the chat reply for that round, next to the other comments. Never write a version into the draft until he picks one.
- What he picks is a keeper line, word for word (coach-gen-writer, step 11).

## Example (3 Nov 2026)

His line: "Before we farmed corn and raised cattle, we were gatherers. For about 300,000 years..."

1. Rhyme in a pair: "Before the plough and the cow, we were gatherers."
2. Rhyme in a pair: "Before farms and barns, we were gatherers."
3. Rhyme in a pair, playful: "Before we kept goats and sowed oats, we were gatherers."
4. Shared vowel: "Before we planted wheat and herded sheep, we were gatherers."

Each can take the second sentence "For about 300,000 years, food was something you walked to, not something you waited for." (alliteration, and it sets up the farmer, who has to wait).
